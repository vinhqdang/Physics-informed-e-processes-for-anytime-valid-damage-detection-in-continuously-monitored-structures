SUMMARY OF THE MAIN CHANGES

1. Calibration and the guarantee. The theorem is now stated as a conditional result, and a new part of Proposition 4 shows what any calibration of the bias allowance must control (a calibration-failure term in the error budget) and what it must assume (that the future is represented by commissioning). The implemented rule (commissioning average plus two standard errors) is described as an empirical calibration with no guarantee of its own. A provably calibrated variant (PHASE-C) is evaluated and found to be too conservative to be useful over a one-year window. Abstract, discussion and conclusions are rewritten accordingly.

2. Proofs. The delay bound (Proposition 5) is re-derived: the inequality direction in the appendix is corrected, the starting-wealth penalty now uses the mixture component to which the drift assumption applies, and the conditioning and stopping assumptions are stated. The identifiability statement (Proposition 1) is generalised and the "if and only if uniform" claim is replaced by a precise statement of when it holds; for the frame studied it does not.

3. New experiments, some unfavourable. (i) Late damage onset: after two healthy years PHASE detects 5% loss in 66% of records (100% with early onset) and 2% loss in none (Table 9). (ii) A restart variant repairs this but loses empirical false-alarm control. (iii) Reanalysis of Z24 with the recorded order of the data shows that the earlier timing advantage of the cone-restricted process over the omnibus process holds only when commissioning epochs from the two reference days are mixed; we no longer claim it as a general finding (Table 16). The directional result (silence on the stiffness recovery) is unaffected. (iv) The conformal martingale with a full-window calibration set holds its level, which changes our interpretation of that comparator.

4. Reporting. Exact intervals for all rates, interquartile ranges, explicit definitions of undetected, pre-onset and localisation denominators, a reconciled record count, a corrected statement on PHASE-BB, full parameter listings and a notation list.

5. Claims proportionate to the evidence (second pass). After the revision was complete we re-examined every headline claim against our own numbers and made further changes. The title no longer says "anytime-valid" and now refers to a lifetime false-alarm budget; the abstract is shortened and states that the budget is conditional; Proposition 4(iii) is restated to say that its representativeness assumption is stronger than envelope coverage and is violated by seasonal bias in the simulated generator, so PHASE-C's zero false alarms are not support for the theorem; arithmetic slips are corrected (the first four record groups sum to 1,800, the healthy monitoring is 1,200 structure-years, the Z24 files hold 700 and 702 epochs); the late-onset table now includes the oracle CUSUM and CTM-F (CUSUM is about eight times faster than PHASE after two healthy years); the Z24 headings no longer say "confirmed", R3 is described as a consequence of the sign restriction, and band-edge peak picking, the spalling baseline and the memoryless-chart timings are reported; ablations show that the physics-derived cone adds power beyond a plain sign restriction while withholding identifiable damage from adaptation has no measurable effect over the horizons simulated; a twin-error experiment (10-20% errors) leaves the results unchanged; earlier subspace-based and sequential-test literature is cited and the "under-exploited" wording softened; and the practice section now covers the choice of alpha, long-lived assets, and the fact that silence is not evidence of safety.

6. Field validation with covariates and masking. A second real bridge with environmental covariates, KW51 (monitored across a retrofit; hourly frequencies, temperature, humidity, wind), now tests the reduced method (Section 5.4, Table 17, Figure 10). The result is negative: with hourly epochs PHASE alarmed before the retrofit works in 11-14 of 15 commissioning configurations for every one of six surrogates, including two nonlinear ones (thermal-inertia regression and random Fourier features); with daily epochs in 0-9 of 15, depending on the surrogate; only the variant with the certified allowance came near controlling false alarms (0-3 of 15), with median delays of 19 to 94 days. The configurations overlap, were not pre-specified, and three surrogates were added after earlier results, so the table is descriptive. A further simulation (Section 4.2.9) shows that a persistent benign stiffening followed 180 days later by a 5% loss leaves the loss undetected by every cone-based monitor (0/100), while the omnibus channel alarms on the stiffening in 100/100 records at a median of 11 days.

7. Literature and claims. Direct comparison with the sequential and environmental-compensation studies suggested, a table distinguishing the error criteria of SPRT, CUSUM, e-detectors and conformal martingales, and a narrowed originality claim.


==================== REVIEWER 1 ====================

PRIORITY 1 / METHODOLOGY 1. Establish the connection between calibration (Section 3.6, commissioning average plus two standard errors) and the conditional score-mean bound assumed by Propositions 3 and 4, including estimation uncertainty, dependence and subsequent environmental variation; otherwise distinguish empirical calibration from the conditional result throughout.

Response. We agree that the commissioning estimate does not establish a uniform bound on future conditional means, and that predictable fitting alone is insufficient. We did two things. First, Proposition 4 now has three parts: (i) the conditional theorem for a known allowance; (ii) for any allowance fixed at the end of commissioning, FWFA <= alpha + P(sigma < infinity), where sigma is the first epoch at which the allowance is too small, so the calibration-failure probability is part of the error budget; (iii) a finite-sample certified allowance based on the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality, which needs no independence between epochs, together with an explicit representativeness assumption (the future conditional excess never exceeds its commissioning average). Estimation uncertainty, serial dependence and later environmental variation enter only through the calibration-failure term and the representativeness assumption, which is the requirement that commissioning spans the operating envelope. Second, we state that the implemented rule has no guarantee of its own: the factor two matches a one-sided error of 2.3% only for independent Gaussian errors, the error is not deducted from the alarm budget, and representativeness cannot be verified from commissioning data. Whether the implemented rule meets the budget is therefore treated as an empirical question. We add a certified variant, PHASE-C, to show the price of a provable statement: no false alarms but almost no power (e.g. 143 of 150 detections of 5% loss at a median of 116 days, none of 2%; Table 6, Table 8). We also examine the sufficient condition directly (Section 4.2.2): the per-epoch bound is not literally met (90-day block means of the excess exceed the allowance in 94.5% of healthy records), yet the discounted process never alarms, so the absence of false alarms must not be read as confirmation of the theorem's condition. The short-commissioning failures of Table 8 are presented as showing why the implementation's guarantee must be qualified.
Changes. Section 3.6 and Proposition 4 (new text and proof in Appendix C); Section 3.5 (conditionality sentence); Section 4.2.2 (assumption diagnostics, PHASE-C); Table 4, Table 8; Abstract (Findings); Section 6 (practice, limitations, conclusions: "valid" used only for the conditional results, "calibrated"/"observed" for implemented behaviour).

PRIORITY 2 / METHODOLOGY 2. The final inequality in the appendix proof of Proposition 5 does not imply equation (13); the drift assumption concerns one mixture component but the penalty uses total mixture wealth; state the conditioning and stopping assumptions.

Response. The reviewer is right on both counts: the appendix inequality had the wrong direction and the penalty used the wrong quantity. Proposition 5 is restated for a fixed bet lambda in the grid, with L_t(lambda) = log of the wealth of that component, the drift assumption on that component, and the bound E[(tau - t*)+ | F_t*] <= (b + B_lambda + (L_t*(lambda))^-) / gamma_lambda, with b = log(1/alpha) + log|Lambda|, so that the penalty is the negative part of the component log-wealth at t*. The proof now uses the mixture-dominates-component inequality, a bounded stopping time T ^ n, optional stopping for the martingale part, the overshoot bound and monotone convergence, in the correct direction. The bound is conditional on F_t* and applies to (tau - t*)+. The corrected penalty turned out to matter in practice: after a long healthy period the high-bet components have lost most of their wealth, which is what the late-onset experiment shows. We also evaluate the bound with plug-in estimates: it held in all 150 records of each of four scenarios, with a median bound about 1.6 times the realised delay (Section 4.2.3).
Changes. Section 3.7, Proposition 5, eq. (13); Appendix C (proof); Section 4.2.3 (bound check); Section 4.2.7.

PRIORITY 2 / METHODOLOGY 3. Reconcile Contribution 2 ("iff uniform") with Proposition 1 (nuisance subspace) and distinguish identifiability relative to nuisance from unique localisation.

Response. Contribution 2 overstated Proposition 1. Proposition 1 now has parts (a)-(d): the confounded set is the subspace K = ker(P_perp A); uniform loss always lies in it; K = span{1} holds when A = D^-1 S has full column rank and the nuisance range contains no direction other than the temperature direction, and fails whenever ker A is not {0} (in particular when there are more substructures than modes) or when a second nuisance direction lies in the range of A; and at most rank(P_perp A) <= m - dim N combinations of damage can be resolved, so a non-zero identifiable fraction is necessary but not sufficient for localisation. For the eight-storey frame (n = 8, m = 6, dim N = 2) the confounded subspace has dimension four, and equal loss at storeys 1 and 8 is confounded exactly (cosine -1.000). We recomputed the map for further nuisance directions (Table 7) and replaced the earlier explanation of the storey-1 localisation failure, which the geometry does not support (Section 4.2.5).
Changes. Contribution 2 (Section 1); Section 3.2, Proposition 1 and following text; Appendix C; Section 4.2.5; Table 7; Conclusions.

PRIORITY 3 / METHODOLOGY 4. Keep validation within scope; carry Section 5.2's qualifications into the abstract and conclusions; justify within-state exchangeability; give chronological or dependence-preserving checks; test later onset; or delimit conclusions.

Response. We did both. (a) The abstract, practice, limitations and conclusion sections now state that Z24 supports only a reduced method and cannot separate damage from environment (Table 14 lists, component by component, what each data set validates). (b) Exchangeability is checked and not assumed (Section 5.2): in the ambient records the median lag-one autocorrelation within a state is 0.02 and 3% of series are significant; in the forced records the median is 0.36 and 70% are significant, so exchangeability there is not supported. The 200 orderings are described as a resampling device for one dataset; the reported standard errors are Monte Carlo errors, not sampling uncertainty about structures. (c) We re-ran all Z24 experiments in the recorded order, in reverse, with random circular shifts and with whole measurement set-ups shuffled (Table 16). Directional discrimination is insensitive to the ordering. The earlier timing result is not: it reproduces when set-ups are shuffled across the two reference days (so serial dependence within a set-up is not the issue) but disappears when the commissioning days are kept apart, because the allowance then rises to 0.58-0.61 from a mean of about 0.06. We therefore withdraw the claim that the cone-restricted process responds sooner to real damage as a general finding. (c') Field data with covariates: the KW51 experiment described above (Section 5.4) is the strongest real-data test we could run with the data available to us; it is a reduced test without a finite element model, and its result is that the reduced method does not reliably control false alarms on a real structure. (d) Late onset: Table 9 places onset at year 3.25. PHASE detects 5% loss in 66 of 100 records (203 days median), none of the 2% losses, and 83 days for 10% (26 days with early onset). A start-time mixture (PHASE-R, Proposition 6) repairs most of the loss but its observed false-alarm probability (0.030 at one year) exceeds the nominal level, because components that start late see only a few months, over which the bias exceeds the commissioning allowance. We report it as a tested remedy with a cost.
Changes. Abstract; Section 5.2 (diagnostics, Table 14, sign-restriction discussion); Section 5.3 (Table 16); Section 5.5; Section 3.8, Proposition 6; Section 4.2.7, Table 9; Section 6.4, Section 6.5.

1. Which components of PHASE are validated by the Z24 data and which only by simulation?

Response. Table 14 answers this component by component. Z24 exercises, in reduced form, the cone (the non-positive orthant), the e-process with the commissioning allowance, and the commissioning-envelope behaviour; KW51 (Section 5.4) adds a reduced test with real covariates (regression surrogate, plain orthant) in which the false-alarm control did not hold; neither data set exercises the physics-structured surrogate (no finite element model, and no covariates in Z24), the nuisance subspace and the identifiability analysis, localisation, or the separation of damage from environment. Section 5.5 states that only the directional result and the dependence on commissioning data survive the additional analyses.
Changes. Section 5.2 (Table 14); Section 5.3; Section 5.5; Abstract; Section 6.4.

2. The cone assumes damage lowers frequencies, whereas the Z24 interventions are settlement, tilt and spalling. Why is the assumption appropriate?

Response. It is a modelling assumption and the data support it only a posteriori. Relative to the reference states, the three tracked frequencies fell in every damage state from 80 mm settlement onwards, in both modalities; only the third mode in the two smallest settlement states rose, by at most 0.6%, comparable to the day-to-day offset (Section 5.2). We do not claim this justifies the assumption in general: mechanisms that raise some frequencies (mass loss, crack closure, boundary stiffening) lie outside the cone and would not be flagged as damage. As implemented, the score is the maximum over singleton, pair and all-mode generators, so a mixed pattern still scores on the falling components. We discuss two mitigations: enlarging the cone with mass-change generators, and running an omnibus process as a separate change-notification channel with its own budget share.
Changes. Section 5.2 (new paragraph on the sign restriction); Section 4.2.4.

3. How would humidity, wind, temperature gradients or changed support conditions affect the identifiability conclusions and the detectability map?

Response. We recomputed the map for a vertical temperature gradient, a support-condition change at storey 1 (equivalently a roof or equipment mass change here) and both (Table 7). Each added direction removes one resolvable dimension (m - dim N) and lowers the identifiable fractions; a support-condition nuisance makes storeys 1 and 8 entirely confounded and storey 2 nearly so. Effects that act as a uniform modulus or mass change fall inside the existing directions. The map is therefore described as an upper bound that must be recomputed for the nuisance set judged plausible for the asset.
Changes. Section 4.2.5, Table 7; Section 3.2; Section 6.1.

4. The future envelope is unknown in practice. How can sufficient commissioning coverage be determined before relying on the guarantee?

Response. It cannot be certified from commissioning data alone, because representativeness concerns the future (Proposition 4). Section 6.2 proposes a protocol: declare the target envelope from independent records (site climatology, occupancy schedule) and measure coverage; estimate the allowance block by block and treat a large spread as a warning; report coverage online and flag alarms outside the envelope; treat the guarantee as inactive until declared coverage is reached; extend commissioning as a restart with its own budget share; use the twin to synthesise unobserved states and state the model risk. A finer commissioning sweep (Table 8) shows that range coverage is necessary but not sufficient (coverage 97-99% at 180-240 days still left the allowance large and power near zero) and that both coverage and the allowance are needed as diagnostics. Gating alone did not restore validity. None of these steps turns representativeness into a theorem; they make failure visible earlier.
Changes. Section 6.2; Section 4.2.6, Table 8, Figure 7; Section 6.1.
