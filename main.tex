\begin{singlespace}
\noindent\textbf{Structured abstract}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Purpose} -- Structures are now monitored continuously, yet damage thresholds are usually set as if a single test were run, so the chance of at least one false alarm over a monitoring life can approach one. This paper develops a monitoring rule whose false-alarm budget applies to a campaign of unspecified length, and examines how far that budget can be trusted once it is calibrated from finite commissioning data.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Design/methodology/approach} -- PHASE (Physics-informed Hedged Anytime-valid Sequential Evidence) predicts how temperature and live load move a structure's natural frequencies with a physics-structured surrogate, scores the remaining discrepancy only in directions that a loss of stiffness can produce (the damage cone), and accumulates the score in a betting e-process, a running measure of evidence that stays valid however long monitoring continues. Evaluation uses 3{,}600 simulated records of an eight-storey frame (2{,}900 two-year and 700 four-year), sixteen comparators and variants, and 690 ambient-vibration epochs (plus 711 forced-vibration epochs) from the Z24 bridge.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Findings} -- The false-alarm guarantee is a conditional result: it holds if the expected score of an undamaged structure stays below a stated bound. The commissioning estimate of that bound used in practice is an empirical calibration, not a proof. With one year of commissioning, PHASE raised no false alarm in 300 undamaged records at a one-year horizon or in 300 at three years (95\% upper limit 1.2\% in each). A $3\sigma$ chart alarmed in every record within days. A 5\% stiffness loss at storey~3 was detected in all 150 records (median delay 59 days); a 2\% loss was detected in only 34\%, and after two healthy years even 5\% loss was detected in only 66\% of records. Commissioning shorter than a season destroyed false-alarm control. On Z24, which has no environmental covariates and no structural model, a reduced version stayed silent when the bridge recovered stiffness, which omnibus detectors flagged, under every ordering of the data tried; its response to settlement depended on how commissioning data were ordered, and those data cannot separate damage from day-to-day environmental change.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Research limitations/implications} -- Validation of the complete framework is simulation-based; the Z24 test supports only the directional-discrimination and commissioning findings. Stiffness-loss patterns lying in the nuisance subspace, including uniform loss, cannot be told apart from temperature or load change using frequencies, and storey-level localisation failed at storey~1 although its identifiable fraction is non-zero.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Practical implications} -- Provided commissioning covers the operating environment and the stated assumptions hold, at most a proportion $\alpha$ of monitored structures raise any false alarm over their whole monitoring life, which turns alarm governance into a budget. Contractual or deployment commitments should remain conditional on that coverage and on the detection delays reported here.}

\rev{\noindent\textbf{Originality/value} -- To the authors' knowledge, this is the first use of betting e-processes with physics-derived damage directions in vibration-based structural health monitoring, and it shows that physics can constrain what is identifiable and not only how the environment is modelled.}

\section{Introduction}

Instrumented buildings and bridges no longer produce measurement campaigns; they produce streams. A modern monitoring installation identifies modal properties automatically, every few hours, indefinitely \citep{mishra2022iot}. The statistical machinery that decides whether those properties indicate damage, however, is largely inherited from an era of episodic testing. A control limit is placed at three standard deviations of a reference population, or a hypothesis test is applied at the 5\% level, and the resulting rule is then evaluated afresh at every epoch for years. The error rate that was nominally controlled is the error rate of a single decision. The error rate that the asset owner actually experiences is the probability that the rule ever fires while the structure is healthy, and under continuous interrogation that probability tends to one.

This is not a subtle effect. In the experiments reported below, a $3\sigma$ chart applied to the \rev{standardised residuals of the same surrogate used by PHASE} raised its first false alarm after a median of two days of monitoring, and did so in every one of 300 undamaged records. A repeatedly applied Hotelling $T^2$ test at the 1\% level behaved identically. Practitioners are of course aware that raw charts alarm too often, and \rev{one common response is} to raise thresholds until alarms become rare in practice. \rev{Where that is done without a stated horizon, it renegotiates the error guarantee silently:} the resulting threshold has no stated operating characteristic, it is specific to the horizon and the data over which it was tuned, and, as this paper shows \rev{for the implementations tested here}, buying false-alarm control this way can extinguish the power to detect the damage the system was installed to find. \rev{This is a statement about the specific charts we implemented, not about every environmental-normalisation method; Section~2.1 discusses the closest engineering studies.}

The consequences run in both directions and both are material for sustainability. Alarms that cannot be trusted are eventually not acted upon, which is the mechanism by which monitoring systems quietly stop informing maintenance decisions \citep{cawley2018gap}; each alarm that is acted upon consumes inspection resources, crew mobilisation, access equipment and embodied carbon. Damage detected late, conversely, forecloses the repair options that preserve embodied carbon and forces replacement. A monitoring rule with a calibrated, horizon-free error guarantee is therefore not a statistical nicety. It is a precondition for monitoring to support the life-extension decisions on which the sustainability case for instrumented infrastructure rests, and it speaks directly to United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 9, 11 and 13.

The statistical tools required to fix this have matured only recently. Safe anytime-valid inference (SAVI) replaces the $p$-value with the e-value, a non-negative statistic whose expectation under the null is at most one, and replaces the fixed-sample test with a non-negative martingale whose value is interpretable as accumulated evidence \citep{ramdas2023game,grunwald2024safe,vovk2021evalues}. Ville's inequality bounds the probability that such a process ever exceeds $1/\alpha$ by $\alpha$, whatever the stopping rule, whatever the horizon, and whether or not the analyst peeks. These methods have been taken up in clinical trials and online experimentation but, to our knowledge, not in vibration-based SHM, which is precisely a setting of unbounded continuous monitoring under optional stopping by a human operator. \rev{Sequential testing is not new to SHM: residual-based sequential probability ratio tests exist \citep{kopsaftopoulos2015sprt}. What is new here is the error criterion (a time-uniform budget for the probability of ever alarming), the nonparametric conditional-mean null, and the physics-derived alternative; Section~2.3 sets out the differences.}

Transplanting the machinery is not, however, sufficient, because SHM's difficulty is not primarily multiplicity. It is that environmental and operational variation (EOV) moves modal properties by more than damage does \citep{sohn2007eov,peeters2001z24}. In the simulations reported here, seasonal and diurnal temperature variation moves the fundamental frequency by roughly $\pm2\%$ while a 5\% stiffness loss at one storey moves it by $0.49\%$. Any monitor is therefore only as good as its model of the nuisance, and a betting process built on a poorly specified residual stream will accumulate evidence against the null for reasons that have nothing to do with damage. This is where physics earns its place, and this paper argues that it earns it in four distinct ways rather than the one usually recognised.

The method developed here, PHASE (Physics-informed Hedged Anytime-valid Sequential Evidence) and sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, uses first-order eigenvalue perturbation of the structure's finite element model to: (i) constrain the EOV surrogate to $m+2$ free parameters, with the temperature response tied across modes by an exact identity, rather than the $5m$ parameters of an unconstrained regression; (ii) restrict the alternative hypothesis to a polyhedral cone of physically admissible directions, since stiffness loss can only reduce frequencies; (iii) determine which stiffness-loss patterns are identifiable \rev{relative to the nuisance} at all, since temperature \rev{and live load reach} the modal set through specific directions and any damage pattern whose signature lies in the span of those directions is confounded with a nuisance change as a matter of structure rather than of statistical power; and (iv) protect identifiable damage from being absorbed by the online adaptation of the nuisance model.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It formulates damage detection under continuous monitoring as an anytime-valid decision problem and gives an alarm rule, PHASE, whose family-wise false-alarm probability over an unbounded monitoring life is at most $\alpha$ \emph{conditionally} on a bound for the expected score (Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}). \rev{It then states precisely what is and is not established for the implemented commissioning-based calibration of that bound: a finite-sample result with an explicit calibration-failure term (Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}), and an empirical assessment of how the implemented rule behaves.}
\item It derives the damage cone and the nuisance sensitivity subspace from eigenvalue perturbation, and proves an identifiability result: a stiffness-loss pattern is confounded with nuisance change if and only if its standardised signature lies in the nuisance subspace (Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}). \rev{Uniform loss is always confounded; it is the only confounded pattern only under additional conditions that the frame studied here does not meet. Identifiability relative to the nuisance is distinguished from unique localisation.} This yields a per-substructure detectability map that tells an engineer which parts of a structure a given modal set can \rev{support claims about}.
\item It prices surrogate misspecification explicitly. If the physics-structured surrogate leaves a residual bias $\delta$, the evidence process is discounted at rate $\log(1+\lambda\delta)$ per epoch and remains valid (Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}); \rev{a corrected delay bound that uses the relevant mixture component} shows how the discount trades against detection speed (Proposition~\ref{prop:delay}).
\item It evaluates the method on real measurements from the Z24 bridge progressive damage test. \rev{Only a reduced version of the method can be run there. The data support the directional discrimination and the dependence of the monitor on its commissioning data; chronological and dependence-preserving re-analyses show that the timing comparison with the omnibus variant depends on how commissioning epochs are ordered.}
\item It reports a controlled simulation study over \rev{3{,}600 simulated records (2{,}900 two-year and 700 four-year)} against sixteen comparators and variants spanning current SHM practice, classical quickest detection and distribution-free anytime-valid testing, \rev{with interval estimates for all rates, a late-onset experiment, a finer commissioning sweep, a finite-sample-certified variant and a restart variant}, and it reports the costs of validity as well as its benefits.
\end{enumerate}

Section~2 reviews the relevant literature. Section~3 formalises the monitoring problem, derives the physics and develops PHASE. Section~4 reports the simulation study, Section~5 the application to the Z24 bridge, and Section~6 discusses implications and concludes.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Vibration-based SHM and environmental variability}\label{sec:related-shm}

The statistical pattern recognition paradigm for SHM \citep{farrar2007intro,farrar2012machine} organises damage identification into operational evaluation, data acquisition, feature extraction and statistical modelling, and its axioms make explicit that damage detection always requires a comparison between two system states \citep{worden2007axioms}. Modal frequencies remain the most widely used global feature because they are cheap to identify from ambient response and are directly interpretable through the eigenvalue problem \citep{doebling1998summary,avci2021review}.

Their weakness is equally well established. Temperature changes the elastic modulus and boundary conditions; live load changes the mass; the resulting modal variation routinely exceeds that caused by structurally significant damage \citep{sohn1999alamosa,sohn2007eov}. Two decades of work on removing these effects have been surveyed recently \citep{wang2022eliminating,han2021temperature}, and a 2025 critical review of baseline compensation, adaptation and reference-free techniques concludes that robustness to environmental and operational variability remains the principal obstacle to deployment \citep{rezazadeh2025eov}. The Z24 bridge campaign, in which a bridge was monitored for a year before progressive artificial damage, remains the canonical demonstration that environmental effects and damage events must be separated before any detection claim is meaningful \citep{peeters2001z24}. Responses in the literature include data normalisation, regression of features on measured covariates, latent-variable methods, and switching response-surface models for structures whose EOV behaviour is regime-dependent \citep{worden2018switching}. What these approaches share is a focus on removing the nuisance; the decision rule applied to the cleaned residual is typically a control limit whose behaviour under repeated application is not analysed.

\begin{revblock}
The closest primary engineering studies deserve a direct comparison. \citet{deraemaeker2008output} combine statistical compensation of environmental effects with monitoring of output-only vibration features, and \citet{maes2022kw51} evaluate regression-based and robust-PCA-based compensation on measurements from the KW51 railway bridge across a retrofit. Related work couples regression, principal-component or cointegration models of the environment with control charts on frequencies or autoregressive coefficients \citep{fugate2001spc,kullaa2003z24,yan2005pca,magalhaes2012arch,cross2012cointegration}. These studies address the question of \emph{how well the environment can be removed}, usually on measured data and with a fixed-sample or fixed-horizon threshold, and they evaluate detection and false-alarm rates empirically. PHASE does not compete with them on environmental compensation: its surrogate is deliberately simple. Its contribution is orthogonal, namely a decision layer whose false-alarm budget is stated over the whole monitoring life and whose alternative is restricted to physically admissible directions. The PCA-EOV chart and the control charts we implement (Section~4.1.3) are single, simple instances of this family, calibrated on simulated healthy campaigns; their poor showing here, in a simulation with deliberately unmodelled thermal lag, gradient and load-proxy error, says nothing about how well carefully tuned compensation behaves on other structures, and \citet{maes2022kw51} report that compensation can work well on real data.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Physics-informed learning and digital twins in the built environment}

Physics-informed machine learning embeds governing equations, symmetries or known operators into learning systems, improving data efficiency and extrapolation \citep{karniadakis2021piml,raissi2019pinn}; a 2025 review traces its adoption in bridge damage detection specifically, characterising it as a grey-box middle ground between physics-based and purely data-driven models \citep{mammeri2025piml}. In civil and construction engineering the same impulse appears under the banner of digital twins, in which a calibrated model of the asset is maintained alongside the physical structure and updated from monitoring data \citep{jiang2021dtcivil,opoku2021dtreview,ferrari2024twins}. Hybrid schemes that combine a physics-based model with machine learning for damage detection have shown clear advantages over purely data-driven pipelines \citep{ritto2021digitaltwin}. Bibliometric surveys of the built-environment literature confirm both the speed of uptake and its fragmentation: applications remain largely task-specific and weakly integrated into whole-asset frameworks \citep{wang2026aidt}, and the sustainability case for these technologies is usually argued at the level of energy and carbon rather than of decision quality \citep{bibri2025aidt}.

Almost all of this work uses physics to improve \emph{estimation}: a better surrogate, a better reduced-order model, better extrapolation. The present paper uses physics additionally to constrain \emph{inference} -- which alternatives are admissible, which are identifiable, and what may be learned online without destroying the detector -- which is a distinct and, we argue, under-exploited role.

\subsection{Sequential monitoring and anytime-valid inference}\label{sec:related-seq}

Sequential change detection has a long history, from the sequential probability ratio test \citep{wald1945sequential} to the CUSUM \citep{page1954cusum} and its optimality properties \citep{lorden1971procedures,moustakides1986optimal,pollak1985optimal}. \rev{These procedures answer different questions, and the differences matter for what a false-alarm guarantee means (Table~\ref{tab:criteria}).}

\begin{revblock}
\noindent\emph{SPRT.} Wald's test decides between two specified hypotheses about the data distribution with prescribed type~I and type~II error probabilities; those probabilities refer to one test run until it stops. A residual-based SPRT framework for vibration monitoring \citep{kopsaftopoulos2015sprt} applies this logic to the residuals of a vibration model and supports decisions among pre-specified damage hypotheses. Restarting the test after each decision does not by itself bound the probability of ever alarming over an unbounded campaign.

\noindent\emph{CUSUM and Shiryaev--Roberts.} These minimise worst-case detection delay subject to a lower bound on the \emph{average run length} to false alarm, and they require the pre- and post-change distributions to be known or accurately estimated. An average-run-length constraint controls a mean, not a probability: if the false-alarm time is roughly geometric with mean $\gamma$, the probability of at least one false alarm within a horizon $h$ is about $h/\gamma$ for small $h/\gamma$ and tends to one thereafter. In SHM practice the pre-change distribution is precisely what is uncertain, because it is the output of a fitted EOV model applied to a non-stationary environment, and thresholds are usually set by simulation (as in our oracle comparators).

\noindent\emph{E-detectors.} E-detectors \citep{shin2023edetectors} are nonparametric change detectors built from e-processes. Like the CUSUM family they sum evidence over candidate change points and their false-alarm guarantee is an average-run-length bound under a composite null; they therefore detect changes quickly without a known post-change law, but they do not provide the time-uniform bound $\Prob_{H_0}(\tau<\infty)\le\alpha$ that PHASE targets.

\noindent\emph{Conformal test martingales.} A conformal test martingale \citep{vovk2021testing} tests whether the whole observation sequence is exchangeable. Ville's inequality bounds the probability that it ever exceeds $1/\alpha$ \emph{under exchangeability}. Its null is therefore a statement about the joint distribution of the sequence rather than about the structure being in its reference condition: any non-exchangeability, such as a seasonal drift in a healthy structure's score, violates the null even though nothing has been damaged.
\end{revblock}

Safe anytime-valid inference offers \rev{the last of these criteria}. An e-value is a non-negative statistic with null expectation at most one; a test martingale is a running product of conditional e-values; Ville's inequality then bounds the probability that the martingale ever exceeds $1/\alpha$ by $\alpha$ \citep{ramdas2023game,grunwald2024safe}. Betting formulations construct these processes as the wealth of a gambler placing predictable bets \citep{waudbysmith2024betting,kelly1956information}, time-uniform confidence sequences arise from the same machinery \citep{howard2021timeuniform}, and e-detectors extend it to change detection \citep{shin2023edetectors}. E-values also combine gracefully: arbitrary dependent e-values may be merged by averaging \citep{vovk2021evalues}, and false discovery rate control across many hypotheses follows directly \citep{wang2022fdr}, which matters when a structure is divided into many monitored zones.

\subsection{Research gap}\label{sec:gap}

Three gaps motivate this work. First, SHM decision rules are evaluated at fixed sample sizes but deployed continuously, and the literature reports few if any measurements of the family-wise false-alarm probability actually experienced over a monitoring life. Second, physics-informed SHM has concentrated on the nuisance model, leaving the admissibility and identifiability of damage alternatives largely implicit. Third, anytime-valid inference has not, \rev{to our knowledge,} been connected to a domain in which its central assumption -- unbounded monitoring with operator-driven stopping -- is not an idealisation but a description of ordinary practice. \rev{Sequential tests for SHM do exist \citep{kopsaftopoulos2015sprt}, as do compensation-plus-monitoring studies \citep{deraemaeker2008output,maes2022kw51}; we are not aware of earlier work that combines a nonparametric e-process with a physics-derived alternative cone and an identifiability-aware adaptation rule, and we make no wider claim of priority.}

\subsection{Monitoring protocol and the anytime-valid decision problem}

A structure is interrogated at epochs $t=1,2,\dots$. At each epoch an automated operational modal analysis returns the first $m$ identified natural frequencies, collected as $y_t=\log \hat f_t\in\R^m$, together with measured covariates $z_t$ comprising ambient temperature deviation $\Delta T_t$ and a live-load proxy $q_t$. Let $\F_t=\sigma(y_1,z_1,\dots,y_t,z_t)$.

A commissioning window $t\le t_0$ is used to calibrate the monitor; monitoring proper runs for $t>t_0$ without a specified end. \rev{A notation and acronym list is given in Appendix~A.} Write $H_0$ for the hypothesis that the structure remains in its reference condition throughout. A monitor is a stopping time $\tau$, the alarm time. The quantity of operational interest is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FWFA}(\alpha) \;=\; \Prob_{H_0}\!\left(\tau<\infty\right),
\label{eq:fwfa}
\end{equation}
the probability that the monitor \emph{ever} alarms on a healthy structure. We require $\mathrm{FWFA}\le\alpha$ for a horizon that is neither fixed nor known in advance. This is a strictly stronger requirement than per-epoch error control, and strictly stronger than average-run-length control, since it constrains the entire monitoring life rather than a rate. \rev{It concerns the first alarm only: after an alarm and an investigation the monitor is restarted, and a restart needs its own share of the budget (Section~6.1).}

\begin{revblock}
\noindent\emph{In words.} An \emph{e-process} is a running tally of evidence against ``the structure is unchanged'', built like a gambler's wealth: at each epoch the monitor bets a fraction of its current wealth that the score will exceed its healthy average. If the structure really is unchanged, the wealth cannot grow on average, so reaching $1/\alpha$ times the starting wealth is very unlikely, and that holds no matter when, or how often, one looks.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Physics: sensitivities, the damage cone, and the nuisance subspace}\label{sec:physics}

Let $M$ be the mass matrix and $K(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_j K_j$ the stiffness matrix, with $K_j$ the contribution of substructure $j$ and $\theta_j$ a multiplier equal to one in the reference state. Writing $\lambda_i=\omega_i^2$ for the eigenvalues and $\phi_i$ for the mode shapes of $K\phi=\lambda M\phi$, first-order perturbation gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \lambda_i}{\partial \theta_j}=\frac{\phi_i^\top K_j \phi_i}{\phi_i^\top M \phi_i},
\qquad
S_{ij}:=\frac{\phi_i^\top K_j \phi_i}{\lambda_i\,\phi_i^\top M \phi_i}\ \ge 0 .
\label{eq:sens}
\end{equation}
Since $\log\omega_i=\tfrac12\log\lambda_i$, a stiffness loss $\rho_j=1-\theta_j\ge0$ at substructure $j$ shifts the log-frequencies by
\begin{equation}
\Delta \log \omega \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,S\rho + O(\|\rho\|^2).
\label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}
Two structural facts follow and are used throughout. First, $S$ is elementwise non-negative: stiffness loss can only lower frequencies. Second, because $K(\theta)$ is homogeneous of degree one in $\theta$, a uniform scaling $\theta=(1-\rho)\mathbf{1}$ scales all eigenvalues equally, whence
\begin{equation}
\textstyle\sum_{j} S_{ij}=1 \quad\text{for every mode } i .
\label{eq:rowsum}
\end{equation}

A uniform change in elastic modulus, which is how temperature principally acts, therefore shifts every log-frequency by the same amount. Writing $\kappa=S\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}$ for the uniform-modulus sensitivity and $\eta_i=\big(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}}\phi_{ik}^2 m_k\big)/(\phi_i^\top M\phi_i)$ for the sensitivity to relative added mass on loaded floors $\mathcal{L}$, the nuisance reaches the modal set only through the two directions $\kappa$ and $\eta$, whatever the functional form of the underlying EOV law.

Let $D=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_m)$ hold the identification scatter of each log-frequency and define standardised quantities $\tilde d_j=-D^{-1}S e_j$. The \emph{damage cone} is the finitely generated convex cone
\begin{equation}
\Cone=\Big\{-D^{-1}S\rho:\ \rho\in\R^n_{+}\Big\}
=\mathrm{cone}\{\tilde d_1,\dots,\tilde d_n\},
\label{eq:cone}
\end{equation}
which is pointed because all its generators lie in the negative orthant. The \emph{nuisance sensitivity subspace} is $\mathcal{N}=\mathrm{span}\{D^{-1}\kappa,\,D^{-1}\eta\}$, with orthogonal projector $P_\perp=I-\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}$.

\begin{revblock}
\noindent\emph{In words.} The damage cone collects every frequency-change pattern that some non-negative loss of stiffness could produce; a change outside it (all frequencies rising, for example) is not damage of that kind. The nuisance subspace collects the frequency-change patterns that temperature and live load can produce. Damage whose signature lies inside that subspace looks, to a frequency-based monitor, like weather or occupancy.
\end{revblock}

\begin{proposition}[Identifiability relative to the nuisance]\label{prop:ident}
Let $A=D^{-1}S$. \rev{(a)} To first order, a stiffness-loss pattern $\rho$ produces a modal signature that is indistinguishable from some combination of a uniform modulus change and a live-load change if and only if $A\rho\in\mathcal{N}$. \rev{These patterns form a linear subspace $\mathcal{K}=\{\rho:P_\perp A\rho=0\}$ of dimension $n-\mathrm{rank}(P_\perp A)\ge n-(m-\dim\mathcal{N})$.}
\rev{(b)} Uniform stiffness loss $\rho\propto\mathbf{1}$ always lies in $\mathcal{K}$.
\rev{(c) If $A$ has full column rank $n$ and the range of $A$ contains no nuisance direction other than $D^{-1}\kappa$ (in particular if temperature is the only nuisance), then $\mathcal{K}=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}$. Conversely, $\mathcal{K}\supsetneq\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}$ whenever $\ker A\ne\{0\}$ (in particular whenever $n>m$) or whenever $\mathcal{N}$ contains a direction in the range of $A$ that is not parallel to $D^{-1}\kappa$.}
\rev{(d)} The identifiable part of the signature of $\rho$ is $P_\perp A\rho$. \rev{Two patterns $\rho,\rho'$ can be told apart from frequencies, to first order, only if $P_\perp A(\rho-\rho')\neq0$; hence at most $\mathrm{rank}(P_\perp A)\le m-\dim\mathcal{N}$ independent combinations of damage can be resolved, and a non-zero identifiable fraction of each substructure is necessary but not sufficient for unique localisation among $n$ substructures.}
\end{proposition}

The proof is given in the Appendix. The practical content is considerable: no monitor, however sophisticated, can separate \rev{a pattern in $\mathcal{K}$, and in particular} a global loss of stiffness, from a warming of the structure using modal frequencies alone, and the ratio
\begin{equation}
\varrho_j \;=\; \frac{\|P_\perp \tilde d_j\|}{\|\tilde d_j\|}\ \in[0,1]
\label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
gives a design-time \emph{detectability map} over the structure: the fraction of substructure $j$'s signature that survives confounding with the nuisance. \begin{revblock}Two cautions follow from parts (c) and (d). First, the statement that thermal confounding occurs only for uniform loss is not true in general: for the eight-storey frame of Section~4.1 ($n=8$, $m=6$, $\dim\mathcal{N}=2$) the confounded subspace has dimension four, and equal stiffness loss at storeys 1 and 8 is confounded exactly (the projected signatures are antiparallel, with cosine $-1.000$ and a numerical residual below $10^{-13}$). Second, $\varrho_j>0$ is identifiability \emph{relative to the nuisance}, not localisability: it says that substructure $j$'s signature is not wholly absorbed by temperature and load, but says nothing about whether it can be distinguished from the signature of substructure $k$. The pairwise cosine $\langle P_\perp\tilde d_j,P_\perp\tilde d_k\rangle/(\|P_\perp\tilde d_j\|\|P_\perp\tilde d_k\|)$ measures how alike the identifiable signatures of single-storey damage are; with only $m-\dim\mathcal{N}=4$ resolvable directions for eight storeys, the eight identifiable signatures cannot be linearly independent.\end{revblock} Section~4.2.5 shows that the map is informative about \rev{detection only weakly and about} localisation \rev{only partly}, for reasons that are themselves instructive.


\subsection{Physics-structured nuisance surrogate with sequential plug-in}

The surrogate predicts reference-condition log-frequencies from covariates. Physics fixes its structure:
\begin{equation}
g_\beta(z_t)_i \;=\; a_i \;+\; b_T\,\kappa_i\,\Delta T_t \;+\; \rev{b_q}\,\eta_i\, q_t ,
\qquad \beta=(a_1,\dots,a_m,b_T,\rev{b_q}),
\label{eq:surrogate}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa_i$ and $\eta_i$ supplied by the digital twin through \eqref{eq:sens}. The model has $m+2$ free scalars; the temperature response is a single scalar \rev{$b_T$} shared across all modes, an exact consequence of \eqref{eq:rowsum}. \rev{(The load coefficient $b_q$ is unrelated to the score-saturation scale $c$ of equation~\eqref{eq:score}.)} The comparator used in the ablations is an unconstrained per-mode quadratic regression on $[1,\Delta T,\Delta T^2,q,\Delta T q]$ with $5m$ free parameters.

Coefficients are updated recursively with forgetting factor $\nu$, and crucially the prediction at epoch $t$ uses $\hat\beta_{t-1}$, estimated from data up to $t-1$ only. The standardised residual
\begin{equation}
r_t \;=\; D^{-1}\big(y_t-g_{\hat\beta_{t-1}}(z_t)\big)
\label{eq:resid}
\end{equation}
is thus a \emph{sequential plug-in} residual, and any statistic formed from $\hat\beta_{t-1}$ is $\F_{t-1}$-measurable. This predictability, not the accuracy of $\hat\beta$, is what preserves the martingale property in Section~3.5.

\subsection{Polyhedral approximation of the damage cone and the score}

For a closed convex cone, $\sup_{v\in\Cone,\|v\|\le1}\langle v,r\rangle=\|\Pi_\Cone(r)\|$. Rather than solving a non-negative least-squares projection at every epoch, PHASE fixes a finite set $V=\{v_1,\dots,v_N\}$ of unit vectors in $\Cone$ -- the normalised generators together with normalised pairwise combinations -- and scores
\begin{equation}
u_t=\max_{k\le N}\langle v_k,r_t\rangle,\qquad
X_t=\min\!\big(\max(u_t,0)/c,\,1\big)\in[0,1].
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Evaluating \eqref{eq:score} is a single $N\times m$ matrix--vector product, which matters for edge deployment on a gateway alongside the sensor array.

\begin{proposition}[Polyhedral approximation]\label{prop:poly}
For every $r$, $\max_k\langle v_k,r\rangle\le\|\Pi_\Cone(r)\|$, and if $V$ has covering radius $\gamma$ in $\Cone\cap\mathbb{S}^{m-1}$ then $\max_k\langle v_k,r\rangle\ \ge\ \|\Pi_\Cone(r)\|-2\sin(\gamma/2)\,\|r\|$. The approximation is monotone: refining $V$ cannot decrease the score.
\end{proposition}

The null hypothesis is stated directly in terms of the score,
\begin{equation}
H_0:\quad \E[X_t\mid \F_{t-1}]\ \le\ \mu_0 \quad\text{for all } t,
\label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_0=\E_{\varepsilon\sim N(0,I_m)}\big[\min(\max_k\langle v_k,\varepsilon\rangle^+/c,1)\big]$ is a design-time constant obtained once by Monte Carlo from the digital twin. Stating the null this way is deliberate: it makes explicit that the surrogate's task is to render \eqref{eq:null} true in the reference condition, and Section~3.6 prices the failure to do so rather than assuming it away. \rev{In words: given everything observed up to the previous epoch, the score of an undamaged structure is on average no larger than the constant $\mu_0$ that the digital twin predicts for ideal, correctly modelled noise. The assumption is about conditional averages of a bounded score; it needs no distributional form and no independence, but it is an assumption about the future, and Section~3.6 explains what it demands of commissioning.}

\subsection{Betting e-process and the alarm rule}\label{sec:eprocess}

For $\lambda\in[0,1/\mu_0)$ define the wealth process $W_0^\lambda=1$ and
\begin{equation}
W_t^\lambda=\prod_{s=t_0+1}^{t}\big(1+\lambda(X_s-\mu_0)\big),
\qquad
W_t=\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}W_t^\lambda ,
\label{eq:wealth}
\end{equation}
mixing over a fixed geometric grid $\Lambda$ so that no bet size need be tuned \citep{waudbysmith2024betting}. The alarm is
\begin{equation}
\tau=\inf\{t> t_0:\ W_t\ge 1/\alpha\}.
\label{eq:alarm}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Anytime validity]\label{prop:valid}
Under \eqref{eq:null}, $(W_t)_{t>t_0}$ is a non-negative supermartingale with $W_{t_0}=1$, and consequently $\Prob_{H_0}(\exists t: W_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$. The bound holds for arbitrary dependence in $(y_t,z_t)$, for any predictable bet sequence, for sequential plug-in estimation of $\hat\beta$, and irrespective of when or how often the operator inspects the process.
\end{proposition}

Two features deserve emphasis. First, no pre-change distribution need be known: only the conditional mean bound \eqref{eq:null} is required, and the score is bounded by construction, so no tail assumption enters. \rev{Proposition~\ref{prop:valid} is a conditional statement: it does not say that the bound \eqref{eq:null} holds for the implemented surrogate, which is the subject of Section~3.6.} Second, $\log W_t$ is directly interpretable as accumulated evidence in nats, so an operator may act on a rising process before it crosses the threshold without invalidating anything -- the guarantee is uniform over stopping rules.

\subsection{Pricing surrogate misspecification and calibrating the allowance}\label{sec:calib}

Real EOV laws are never captured exactly. Thermal lag, vertical temperature gradients and imperfect load proxies leave a residual bias, so that \eqref{eq:null} holds only up to some $\delta\ge0$. \rev{Write $m_t=\E[X_t\mid\F_{t-1}]-\mu_0$ for the conditional mean excess of the score, an $\F_{t-1}$-measurable quantity, and let $\hat\delta\ge0$ be an allowance that is fixed at the end of commissioning ($\F_{t_0}$-measurable). Define the discounted component wealth $\widetilde W^\lambda_t=\prod_{s=t_0+1}^{t}\frac{1+\lambda(X_s-\mu_0)}{1+\lambda\hat\delta}$ and the mixture $\widetilde W_t=|\Lambda|^{-1}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\widetilde W^\lambda_t$.}

\begin{proposition}[Bias discount and its calibration]\label{prop:delta}
\begin{revblock}
\noindent(i) \emph{Known allowance.} If $m_t\le\delta$ for all $t>t_0$ and $\hat\delta=\delta$ is deterministic, $\widetilde W^\lambda$ and $\widetilde W$ are non-negative supermartingales and $\Prob(\exists t:\widetilde W_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$.

\noindent(ii) \emph{Estimated allowance.} For any $\F_{t_0}$-measurable $\hat\delta$, let $\sigma=\inf\{t>t_0:m_t>\hat\delta\}$ be the first epoch at which the allowance is too small. Then
\[
\Prob(\exists t:\widetilde W_t\ge1/\alpha)\ \le\ \alpha+\Prob(\sigma<\infty).
\]
The second term is the calibration-failure probability and belongs in the total error budget.

\noindent(iii) \emph{A finite-sample certified allowance.} Let $C$ be the $n$ epochs of the second half of commissioning, whose scores are sequential plug-in scores. Assume \emph{representativeness}: $\sup_{t>t_0}m_t\le\bar m_C:=n^{-1}\sum_{s\in C}m_s$ almost surely, that is, the future conditional excess never exceeds its average over commissioning. For $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ put $\hat\delta_\varepsilon=\big(\bar X_C-\mu_0+\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)/(2n)}\big)^{+}$. Then $\Prob(\sigma<\infty)\le\varepsilon$, so the alarm rule with threshold $1/(\alpha-\varepsilon)$ and allowance $\hat\delta_\varepsilon$ has $\mathrm{FWFA}\le\alpha$, whatever the dependence between epochs.
\end{revblock}
\end{proposition}

\begin{revblock}
Proposition~\ref{prop:delta} separates three statements that are easily conflated. Part~(i) is the conditional theorem: validity given a uniform bound on the conditional excess. Part~(ii) shows that a data-dependent allowance converts that theorem into a bound with an additional term, the probability that the allowance is ever too small. Part~(iii) shows what it takes to control that term: a concentration inequality for the commissioning average that does not need independence (Azuma--Hoeffding, since the scores lie in $[0,1]$ and are plug-in), and an assumption linking the future to the commissioning period. That assumption, representativeness, is exactly the requirement that commissioning spans the operating envelope, including the seasonal and operational variation that follows; no procedure can verify it from commissioning data alone, and Section~4.2.6 shows what happens when it fails.

\paragraph{What the implemented rule establishes.} PHASE as implemented estimates the allowance as $\hat\delta=(\bar X-\mu_0+2\,\widehat{\mathrm{se}})^{+}$ from the second half of the commissioning window, with $\widehat{\mathrm{se}}$ the sample standard error of the plug-in scores, and discounts the evidence at $\log(1+\lambda\hat\delta)$ nats per epoch. This is a calibration heuristic. The factor two corresponds to a one-sided error of about 2.3\% only if the scores were independent and the sample mean Gaussian, the standard error ignores serial dependence (the scores are positively autocorrelated, Section~4.2.2), the error is not subtracted from the alarm budget, and representativeness is untested. Proposition~\ref{prop:delta} therefore does \emph{not} establish that the implemented procedure has $\mathrm{FWFA}\le\alpha$. What it establishes is the conditional statement of part~(i) and the form that any calibration guarantee must take; whether the implemented rule meets the budget is an empirical question, answered for the simulated scenarios in Section~4.2.2 and for short commissioning windows in Section~4.2.6. To show the price of a provable statement we also evaluate PHASE-C, which uses part~(iii) with $\varepsilon=\alpha/2$ and the threshold $2/\alpha$; its guarantee is conditional on representativeness and nothing else.

Throughout the paper, ``valid'' therefore means \emph{conditionally valid} when it refers to Propositions~\ref{prop:valid} and~\ref{prop:delta}, and ``calibrated'' or ``observed'' when it refers to the false-alarm frequencies of the implemented rule. Estimation uncertainty, serial dependence and later environmental variation enter the guarantee only through $\Prob(\sigma<\infty)$ and the representativeness assumption.
\end{revblock}

This converts model quality into a currency: a surrogate that leaves half the bias buys back half the discount, and the mechanism gives a precise sense in which better physics is worth more than better statistics.

\subsection{Detection delay}\label{sec:delaybound}

\begin{proposition}[Delay bound]\label{prop:delay}
\begin{revblock}
Fix $\lambda\in\Lambda$ and a change time $t^\star\ge t_0$. Let $L^\lambda_t=\log\widetilde W^\lambda_t$ be the log-wealth of the discounted component with bet $\lambda$, and suppose that for all $s>t^\star$, almost surely,
\[
\E\big[\log(1+\lambda(X_s-\mu_0))\mid\F_{s-1}\big]-\log(1+\lambda\hat\delta)\ \ge\ \gamma_\lambda\ >\ 0 .
\]
Let $b=\log(1/\alpha)+\log|\Lambda|$, let $B_\lambda=\max\{0,\log(1+\lambda(1-\mu_0))-\log(1+\lambda\hat\delta)\}$ bound the increments of $L^\lambda$ from above, and write $x^-=\max(-x,0)$. Then the alarm time $\tau$ of the mixture rule satisfies
\begin{equation}
\E\big[(\tau-t^\star)^{+}\mid\F_{t^\star}\big]\ \le\ \frac{b+B_\lambda+\big(L^\lambda_{t^\star}\big)^{-}}{\gamma_\lambda}.
\label{eq:delay}
\end{equation}
If the drift condition holds for several $\lambda$, the bound holds for each and the smallest may be taken.
\end{revblock}
\end{proposition}

\begin{revblock}
The bound has the expected shape: delay grows logarithmically in $1/\alpha$ and in the size of the bet grid, and inversely in the evidence growth rate $\gamma_\lambda$, from which the bias discount subtracts directly ($\gamma_\lambda=g(\lambda)-\log(1+\lambda\hat\delta)$ when $g(\lambda)$ lower-bounds the undiscounted log-growth). Two points deserve emphasis. The drift condition concerns one component, so the starting-wealth penalty must use that component's own log-wealth $L^\lambda_{t^\star}$, not the total mixture wealth; the two can differ greatly, because after a long healthy period the components with large bets have lost most of their wealth while the mixture is carried by components with small bets. And the penalty $(L^\lambda_{t^\star})^-$ is the quantitative form of ``evidence lost during a long healthy period'': it enters the delay one-for-one at rate $1/\gamma_\lambda$. Section~4.2.7 tests this with late damage onset, and compares the bound with realised delays.
\end{revblock}
Restricting the alternative to the cone raises $g(\lambda)$ by removing the noise that an omnibus statistic must accumulate over all directions, which is why the cone ablation in Section~4 loses so much power.

\begin{revblock}
\subsection{Restarts: protecting against evidence lost in long healthy periods}\label{sec:restart}

The penalty $(L^\lambda_{t^\star})^-$ in \eqref{eq:delay} has a practical consequence. During a long healthy period the evidence process drifts downwards: the allowance $\hat\delta$ is subtracted at every epoch, and the logarithm of a bet has a negative drift (Jensen) even when the conditional mean excess is zero. The components with large bets, which are the ones that detect damage quickly, lose the most. A change that starts late is then detected slowly, however large it is. The remedy is to hedge over the start time of the evidence process as well as over the size of the bet.

\begin{proposition}[Start-time mixture]\label{prop:restart}
Let $s_0<s_1<\dots$ be fixed start times with $s_0=t_0$ and let $\pi_k\ge0$ with $\sum_k\pi_k\le1$. Let $\widetilde W^{(k)}_t=1$ for $t\le s_k$ and, for $t>s_k$, let $\widetilde W^{(k)}_t$ be the mixture over $\Lambda$ of $\prod_{s_k<u\le t}(1+\lambda(X_u-\mu_0))/(1+\lambda\hat\delta)$. Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}(i), $W^R_t=\sum_k\pi_k\widetilde W^{(k)}_t$ is a non-negative supermartingale with initial value $\sum_k\pi_k\le1$, so $\Prob(\exists t:W^R_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$. If the change occurs at $t^\star$ and $k^\star=\max\{k:s_k\le t^\star\}$, the bound \eqref{eq:delay} holds for the alarm time of $W^R$ with $b$ replaced by $b+\log(1/\pi_{k^\star})$ and $L^\lambda_{t^\star}$ replaced by the log-wealth of the component started at $s_{k^\star}$ and observed up to $t^\star$.
\end{proposition}

PHASE-R, evaluated below, starts a new evidence process every 90 days (360 epochs) with weights $\pi_k=1/((k+1)(k+2))$, which sum to one. The price is the extra $\log(1/\pi_k)\approx2\log(k+2)$ nats in the numerator of \eqref{eq:delay}, which grows slowly with the age of the installation; the gain is that the deficit term is bounded by what accumulates in at most 90 days. The construction is in the spirit of change-point maximisation in e-detectors \citep{shin2023edetectors} but keeps the time-uniform probability bound, because the components are averaged and not maximised. Like Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}, validity is conditional on the bound on the conditional mean excess, and the same representativeness caveat applies.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Identifiability-aware adaptation}\label{sec:adapt}

Online adaptation of a nuisance model in a damage detector is dangerous: with forgetting factor $\nu$, the surrogate absorbs a persistent step within roughly $1/(1-\nu)$ epochs, and a detector whose reference model chases the damage will eventually go quiet. Freezing the surrogate after commissioning avoids this but forfeits tracking of genuine long-term drift, and in preliminary experiments a naive evidence-gated freeze produced a positive feedback loop in which a frozen surrogate generated ever-larger residuals.

Physics resolves the tension. Let $\tilde V$ denote the generators of the cone projected onto $\mathcal{N}^\perp$ and normalised, and let $w_t=\max_k\langle\tilde v_k,r_t\rangle^{+}\,\tilde v_{k^\star}$ be the identifiable damage-consistent component of the residual. The surrogate is updated towards the target
\begin{equation}
y_t - D\big(r_t-\mathrm{clip}(r_t-w_t,\pm\varsigma)\big),
\label{eq:filtered}
\end{equation}
that is, the identifiable damage-consistent component is withheld from adaptation while everything else, Huber-clipped at $\varsigma$, is retained. Because the nuisance acts within $\mathcal{N}$ and the withheld component lies in $\mathcal{N}^\perp$, the surrogate remains free to learn the entire temperature and live-load response -- which it must, since temperature acts inside the damage cone -- while identifiable damage cannot be absorbed. The rule is a fixed function of $r_t$ and so preserves predictability.

\subsection{Localisation and multiple zones}

The generator attaining the maximum in \eqref{eq:score} carries a support over substructures. Accumulating $L_j \mathrel{+}= \mathrm{supp}_j(v_{k^\star})\,(X_t-\mu_0)^{+}$ over the monitoring period yields a localisation index whose argmax names the implicated substructure. \rev{The index is a heuristic accumulator, not a calibrated estimator, and carries no error guarantee of its own.} For structures partitioned into zones, a separate e-process per zone can be run and the zone-level processes merged by averaging, which is valid under arbitrary dependence \citep{vovk2021evalues}; if zone-level claims are wanted, e-values support false discovery rate control directly \citep{wang2022fdr}, avoiding the conservatism of a Bonferroni correction across zones.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{PHASE monitoring cycle (one epoch)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Design time:} $S,\kappa,\eta$ from the twin; generators $V,\tilde V$; projector $P_\perp$; $\mu_0$ by Monte Carlo
\State \textbf{Commissioning:} fit $\hat\beta$; estimate $D$; estimate $\hat\delta$ on \rev{the second half of the commissioning epochs, scored by the sequential plug-in rule}
\For{$t>t_0$}
  \State $r_t \gets D^{-1}(y_t-g_{\hat\beta_{t-1}}(z_t))$ \Comment{sequential plug-in}
  \State $u_t\gets\max_k\langle v_k,r_t\rangle$; \ $X_t\gets\min(u_t^{+}/c,1)$
  \State $\log W^\lambda_t \gets \log W^\lambda_{t-1}+\log(1+\lambda(X_t-\mu_0))-\log(1+\lambda\hat\delta)$ for $\lambda\in\Lambda$
  \State \textbf{if} $\mathrm{logmeanexp}_\lambda \log W^\lambda_t\ \ge\ \log(1/\alpha)$ \textbf{then} raise alarm; report localisation index
  \State update $\hat\beta$ towards the cone-filtered target \eqref{eq:filtered}
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:sim}

\subsection{Experimental design}

\subsubsection{Digital twin and data generation}

The test structure, drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:frame}, is an eight-storey shear frame with storey mass $2.5\times10^5$\,kg and storey stiffness $6.0\times10^{8}$\,N/m, giving reference frequencies of 1.44, 4.27, 6.95, 9.40, 11.52 and 13.26\,Hz for the $m=6$ identified modes. Epochs are six hours apart, corresponding to automated modal identification four times per day, and records span two years (2{,}920 epochs) with a one-year commissioning window unless stated otherwise.


\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_frame.png}
\caption{The simulated structure. (a) Eight-storey shear frame with lumped storey masses and storey stiffnesses; damage is introduced as a multiplicative stiffness loss at a single storey, storey~3 unless stated otherwise. (b) Mode shapes and natural frequencies of the reference state, from which the sensitivity matrix $S$ of equation~\eqref{eq:sens} is computed.}
\label{fig:frame}
\end{figure}

Environmental forcing follows a northern-Vietnam monsoon calendar: an annual temperature cycle of $24\pm7^\circ$C, a diurnal cycle of amplitude $4^\circ$C, and autocorrelated weather noise, giving an ambient range of roughly $10$--$39^\circ$C. Three features are deliberately \emph{not} available to the monitor. Storey temperatures follow ambient through first-order thermal lags with storey-dependent time constants and a vertical gradient spanning $\pm15\%$; the live load acts on floors 2--7 with an occupancy and weekday cycle and is observed only through a proxy with 10\% multiplicative error; and modal identification noise is Student-$t$ with five degrees of freedom, scaled to 0.60--1.20\% relative standard deviation across modes. The monitor sees only single-sensor ambient temperature and the load proxy, so its surrogate is misspecified in exactly the way field surrogates are. \rev{All settings of the generator, the monitors and the comparators are listed in Appendix~B.}

Modulus depends on temperature with $\beta_T=2.5\times10^{-3}$\,$^\circ$C$^{-1}$. Frequencies are computed exactly by solving the generalised eigenproblem at each epoch, not by the perturbation formula used by the monitor, so the first-order model is genuinely approximate from the monitor's point of view.

\subsubsection{Scenarios}

Seven non-healthy scenarios are simulated, each with onset at epoch 1{,}825 (year 1.25): abrupt stiffness loss of 2\%, 5\% and 10\% at storey~3; a gradual loss reaching 6\% at storey~3 over 60 days; abrupt 5\% loss at storeys 1 and 6, chosen for their contrasting identifiable fractions; and a benign 3\% \emph{stiffening} of all storeys, representing temporary propping during façade works, which must not trigger a damage alarm. Undamaged records are simulated over two years and over four years to test behaviour beyond a calibration horizon. \rev{Two further groups were added to answer questions about commissioning and onset time: a finer sweep of the commissioning window, and records of four years with damage onset late, at epoch 4{,}745 (year 3.25), after two years of healthy monitoring.}

\subsubsection{Comparators}\label{sec:comparators}

All comparators consume the same residual stream where possible, so that differences reflect the decision rule rather than the features. \rev{Variant labels are used identically in text, tables and figures. \textbf{PHASE} is the full method with the commissioning-estimated allowance $\hat\delta$ of Section~3.6. \textbf{PHASE-ND} omits the allowance ($\hat\delta=0$; ``no discount''). \textbf{PHASE-C} uses the finite-sample certified allowance of Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}(iii) with $\varepsilon=\alpha/2$ and the alarm threshold $2/\alpha$. \textbf{PHASE-$\Omega$} replaces the cone score with the omnibus norm $\|r_t\|$, retaining all other machinery. \textbf{PHASE-BB} replaces the physics-structured surrogate with the unconstrained per-mode regression. \textbf{PHASE-E} additionally suspends betting when covariates fall outside the commissioning envelope.}

Four comparators represent current SHM practice. The \textbf{$3\sigma$ chart} alarms when any $|r_{it}|>3$. The \textbf{calibrated chart} uses a threshold set at the 99th percentile of the healthy maximum over a one-year monitoring horizon, obtained from 150 independent healthy simulations. The \textbf{Mahalanobis novelty index} is the classical outlier statistic $\sqrt{r_t^\top\hat\Sigma^{-1}r_t}$ with a threshold calibrated the same way rather than taken from a $\chi^2$ table. The \textbf{PCA-EOV chart} implements one standard data-normalisation strategy directly: the leading two principal components of the commissioning log-frequency matrix, which are dominated by environmental response, are projected out and the norm in the retained subspace is charted, again with a simulation-calibrated threshold. The \textbf{repeated $T^2$ test} applies a $\chi^2_m$ test at the 1\% level every epoch. \rev{These are single, simple implementations, not the best that tuned compensation can achieve.}

Three comparators represent classical sequential detection, all applied to the same cone score and all with thresholds calibrated to a one-year horizon on 150 healthy simulations: \textbf{CUSUM}, the \textbf{Shiryaev--Roberts} procedure, and an \textbf{EWMA} chart. These are oracles in a strong sense, since they require 150 replicated realisations of the true healthy process, which no real deployment possesses.

\begin{revblock}
Finally, two \textbf{conformal test martingales} are anytime-valid comparators that obtain their guarantee differently from PHASE. Cone scores from a \emph{fixed} calibration set serve as the reference; each monitoring epoch yields a smoothed conformal $p$-value $p_t$ (the rank of $X_t$ among the calibration scores, ties broken at random), and a mixture of power martingales $\int_0^1\prod_s \epsilon p_s^{\epsilon-1}\,\mathrm{d}\epsilon$, approximated on a grid of twelve values of $\epsilon$ in $[0.05,0.95]$, accumulates evidence with the same Ville alarm at $1/\alpha$ \citep{vovk2021testing}. The first (\textbf{CTM}) uses the second half of the commissioning window as the calibration set, as PHASE does for $\hat\delta$; the second (\textbf{CTM-F}) uses scores from the whole commissioning window, which spans a full seasonal cycle. Both use the identical score and alarm rule, so the comparison isolates one question: whether validity should rest on exchangeability of the calibration sample or, as in PHASE, on a conditional mean bound with an explicit misspecification discount.

The validity of these comparators rests on a specific assumption, which we state because it determines when their guarantee should be expected to hold. The $p_t$ are uniform and independent, and the product is a test martingale, if the monitoring scores are exchangeable with the calibration scores and the calibration set is treated as fixed. The intended reference condition is therefore that the healthy structure's score sequence is stationary and free of serial dependence, so that commissioning and monitoring scores come from one distribution. The simulated healthy structure does not meet that condition in two ways that real structures also fail: the scores are serially dependent (thermal lag and weather persistence; Section~4.2.2 reports their autocorrelation), and the surrogate that generates them keeps adapting. A failure of the guarantee for these comparators is therefore a failure of their stated null in this setting and not an error in Ville's inequality.
\end{revblock}

\subsubsection{Metrics and accounting}\label{sec:metrics}

We report the family-wise false-alarm probability \eqref{eq:fwfa} over one- and three-year monitoring horizons; detection rate and detection delay after onset; pre-onset false alarms in damage scenarios; and localisation accuracy. \rev{Rates are reported as counts with exact (Clopper--Pearson) 95\% confidence intervals, which are informative even when no event is observed: zero events in $n$ records give an upper limit of $1-0.025^{1/n}$, that is 1.2\% for $n=300$ and 0.6\% for $n=600$.} \rev{These intervals describe sampling variability across simulated structures drawn from one generator; they are not statements about other generators or about real structures.}

\begin{revblock}
The remaining quantities are defined as follows, because the treatment of records that never alarm, or that alarm too early, changes the numbers.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Pre-onset alarm.} An alarm at an epoch before damage onset. Such a record is a false alarm in the damage scenario; it is counted in the false-alarm column and is \emph{not} counted as a detection. Where the number of such records is non-zero it is stated.
\item \emph{Detection and detection rate.} A record is detected if its first alarm falls at or after onset and within the remaining nine months of the record (1{,}095 epochs after onset, 4$\times$274 days). The detection rate is the number of detected records divided by all records of the scenario.
\item \emph{Undetected record.} No alarm in the whole record. Undetected records are right-censored at the end of the monitoring window.
\item \emph{Detection delay.} Days from onset to first alarm. Medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) are computed \emph{among detected records only}, so they are conditional on detection; when the detection rate is below one half the unconditional median is undefined (the median record is censored). A conditional median should be read together with the detection rate.
\item \emph{Localisation.} The localisation index is read at the PHASE alarm epoch. Accuracy is the fraction of \emph{detected} records in which the index names the damaged storey; its denominator is the number of detected records, stated beside the percentage.
\end{itemize}

The more interesting comparisons are with the methods that do control error to some degree. Given 150 replications of the true healthy process, the oracle-calibrated chart holds to 0.007 over the horizon for which it was tuned, but its false-alarm probability then grows as monitoring continues, reaching 0.023 at three years (Figure~\ref{fig:fwer}). The Mahalanobis novelty index behaves identically, and the PCA-EOV chart -- \rev{our implementation of one standard data-normalisation strategy} -- is worse at both horizons, at 0.017 and 0.040 \rev{(the three-year interval, 0.021--0.069, excludes 0.01)}. The distinction is not a matter of a better-chosen threshold: a threshold calibrated for a horizon is valid only at that horizon, whereas a supermartingale threshold is valid at all of them simultaneously, \rev{given its conditional assumption}.

\begin{revblock}
The conformal test martingale needs a careful reading. With the second half of the commissioning window as calibration set (CTM) it exceeded the nominal level, at 0.033 and 0.030 (intervals 0.016--0.060 and 0.014--0.056). With a calibration set drawn from the whole commissioning window (CTM-F) there was no false alarm in 300 records at either horizon. The excess of CTM is therefore not evidence that conformal calibration cannot control error. It reflects a calibration set that covers only half a seasonal cycle and is not exchangeable with the monitoring scores, which is the assumption its guarantee needs (Section~\ref{sec:comparators}). The same lesson applies to PHASE: its theorem needs the allowance to represent the future, and Section~\ref{sec:res-comm} shows what happens when commissioning does not span the cycle. What distinguishes the two is the form of the assumption (exchangeability of the score sequence for CTM; a conditional mean bound with an explicit allowance for PHASE), and, as Table~\ref{tab:delay} shows, power: CTM-F detected 3\% of 2\% losses where PHASE detected 34\%, and was slower at 5\% loss.

The value of the allowance is visible in the same table. Without it (PHASE-ND), the false-alarm probability was 0.003 at one year and 0.017 at three (interval 0.005--0.038), as the unmodelled thermal lag and gradient accumulated evidence at a slow but non-zero rate. With $\hat\delta$ estimated from commissioning data (mean 0.020, standard deviation 0.011 across the 150 calibration records) no false alarm occurred in 600 healthy records totalling roughly 1{,}300 structure-years of monitoring. The certified allowance of PHASE-C (mean 0.059) also gave no false alarm, at a large cost in power (Table~\ref{tab:delay}).

\paragraph{How well do the assumptions hold in the simulation?} The conditional mean excess $m_t$ cannot be observed, but its time averages can be estimated from the healthy records. Over the 600 healthy records the median monitoring-period mean of $X_t-\mu_0$ was $-0.003$, against a median allowance of 0.020, and it exceeded the allowance in 8 records (1.3\%). The lag-one autocorrelation of the score was weak (median 0.04; 10th to 90th percentile 0.01--0.06). The 90-day block means, however, exceeded the allowance in 94.5\% of the records. The uniform bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}(i), which is sufficient for validity, is therefore not literally satisfied at the scale of a season: the bias of the surrogate varies seasonally and diurnally. The discounted process still never alarmed, because over a whole campaign the negative drift of the log-wealth (the allowance plus the Jensen term) outweighs short stretches of positive excess. The absence of false alarms is thus not evidence that the theorem's condition holds; it shows slack in the allowance for this generator, and the restart variant (Section~\ref{sec:res-late}), whose components see only a few months, shows that the slack is limited.
\end{revblock}

The results describe a frontier rather than a winner, and the costs of validity should be stated plainly. Every method that detects faster than PHASE pays for it somewhere. The oracle CUSUM and Shiryaev--Roberts procedures detect roughly twice as fast throughout and hold their error rate, but they are calibrated from 150 replications of the true healthy process and their guarantee is an average run length rather than a probability over the monitoring life; neither is available to a real deployment. The EWMA chart is faster still, at a median of 11 days for 5\% damage, but its false-alarm probability reaches 0.020 by three years and it detects only 17\% of 2\% losses. The conformal martingale with the half-window calibration set detects 5\% damage in 45 days against PHASE's 59, and 79\% of 2\% losses against PHASE's 34\%, but at three times the nominal false-alarm rate; with the full-window calibration set (CTM-F) it holds the level but is slower than PHASE at 5\% loss (71 against 59 days) and for gradual loss, similar at 10\% loss, and detects only 3\% of 2\% losses. \rev{PHASE, PHASE-BB, PHASE-E and CTM-F all combine zero observed false alarms at both horizons with detection of every loss of 5\% or more. PHASE is therefore not unique in this respect. PHASE-BB and PHASE-E are variants of the same framework, and CTM-F consumes the same cone score. PHASE's distinguishing features are its stated conditional guarantee, its higher power for small losses than CTM-F, and, through the cone, its discrimination of benign change; it has no advantage over PHASE-BB once a full year of commissioning is available.}

The discount is part of that price: undiscounted PHASE (PHASE-ND) detects 5\% loss in 43 days rather than 59, and 95\% of 2\% losses rather than 34\%, while drifting above the nominal level. The certified variant pays far more: PHASE-C detected 143 of 150 losses of 5\% at a median of 116 days and none of the 2\% losses. Proposition~\ref{prop:delay} makes the exchange rate explicit. \begin{revblock}Evaluating the bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:delay} with the realised log-growth rate of each record as $\gamma_\lambda$ (a plug-in illustration, not a test of the drift condition) and the realised component log-wealth at onset, the bound held in all 150 records of each of the four scenarios examined (5\% and 10\% at storey 3, 5\% at storeys 1 and 6), with a median bound about 1.6 times the realised delay; for 5\% loss at storey 3 the median bound was 94 days against an observed median of 59.\end{revblock}

At the other end of the frontier sit the two data-normalisation comparators. The PCA-EOV chart and the Mahalanobis index detected at most 2\% of damaged records in every scenario while also failing to control error beyond their calibration horizon -- the worst of both, and a caution against reading a quiet chart as a well-behaved monitor, \rev{although these are single implementations whose thresholds were calibrated on simulated healthy campaigns}.

Two further observations concern the stiffening scenario. The EWMA, CUSUM, Shiryaev--Roberts and conformal comparators all remained silent there, but this is inherited rather than earned: each consumes the physics-derived cone score, and the directional restriction is what discards the change. Only PHASE-$\Omega$, which sees the omnibus norm, alarms. The physics, not the sequential machinery, supplies the discrimination.

The horizon-calibrated chart illustrates the opposite failure. Its threshold had to reach 24.3 standardised units to survive the EOV-contaminated residual stream, and it consequently detected 5\% damage in none of 150 records and 10\% damage in 1.3\%. Controlling false alarms by raising a threshold, rather than by accumulating evidence, sacrifices essentially all of the detection capability the monitoring system was installed to provide. \subsubsection{Discriminating benign change: the value of the damage cone}\label{sec:res-cone}

The clearest single result concerns the stiffening scenario, in which temporary propping raises all storey stiffnesses by 3\%. This is a real change in the structure, and any omnibus change detector should and does find it: PHASE-$\Omega$ alarmed in all 150 records, at a median of 11 days. PHASE alarmed in none, because the change lies outside the damage cone -- frequencies rose, and stiffness loss cannot raise frequencies. The oracle CUSUM, which in our implementation consumes the same cone score, also correctly stayed silent, which underlines that the discriminating ingredient is the physics-derived restriction of the alternative and not the sequential machinery. \rev{That discrimination is a consequence of the sign assumption: a damage mechanism that raised some frequencies would be treated in the same way (Section~\ref{sec:z24-scope}).}

The cone contributes power as well as specificity. Removing it while holding everything else fixed reduced the detection rate for 5\% damage from 1.00 to 0.29 and roughly tripled the median delay (Table~\ref{tab:delay}). An omnibus statistic must accumulate noise over all $m$ directions; the cone restricts attention to the ones damage can actually produce.

\subsubsection{The detectability map: what physics can and cannot promise}\label{sec:res-detect}

Figure~\ref{fig:detect} shows the identifiable fraction \eqref{eq:retention} for each storey. It ranges from 0.245 at storey 1 to 0.588 at storey 6: between 41\% and 76\% of each storey's modal signature is confounded with the nuisance directions and is unavailable for inference, however long one monitors.

The map's empirical content is more subtle than we anticipated, and the discrepancy is worth reporting. Detection delay did \emph{not} follow the identifiable fraction: 5\% loss at storey 1, whose identifiable fraction is lowest, was detected fastest (median 38 days) because its \emph{total} signature norm is largest (57.5 versus 42.2 standardised units for storey 3). The resolution is that the surrogate absorbs the confounded component only over its adaptation timescale, roughly 2{,}000 epochs here, which is longer than the detection delays observed. Over practical horizons detection is therefore governed by the full signature; the identifiable fraction governs the asymptotic regime.

\begin{revblock}
Localisation does not follow the map either, and the obvious explanation of it does not survive scrutiny. With the index read at the alarm epoch, damage at storeys 3 and 6 was localised correctly in 150 of 150 and 149 of 150 detected records, and 2\% damage at storey 3 in 46 of 51. Damage at storey 1 was localised correctly in none of 150: it was attributed to storey 3 in 61 records, to storey 6 in 56, to storey 7 in 18, to storey 2 in 9 and to storey 4 in 6. One might attribute this to the storeys whose identifiable directions best match the residual once the confounded component is removed. That is not what the geometry says: in the idealised limit in which the nuisance-aligned component of the storey-1 signature is fully absorbed, the cone generator nearest to the remaining signature is storey 1's own, and the pairwise cosines of the identifiable signatures of storey 1 with storeys 3 and 6 are low (0.27 and 0.17). The failure must therefore come from finite-sample noise, from partial absorption by the adaptive surrogate, and from the index itself. The last point can be checked: on the 300 undamaged records the index, read at the end of the record, named storey 6 in 99, storey 8 in 95, storey 7 in 46, storey 3 in 44, storey 2 in 13 and storey 4 in 3 records, and never storeys 1 or 5. The index has a baseline preference for some storeys independent of damage, and storey 6 is its most frequent healthy winner; the correct localisation at storey 6 should be read with that in mind, and a debiased index would be needed before localisation claims could be made. The identifiable signatures of storeys 1 and 8 are exactly antiparallel (cosine $-1.000$), a case of the confounding in Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}(c).
\end{revblock}
Detection and localisation are distinct capabilities, and \rev{identifiability relative to the nuisance, which the map reports, predicts neither of them reliably in this study.} For asset managers this is still actionable, but in a weaker form than the map alone suggests: before any sensor is installed the map says which substructures \rev{lose most of their signature to the nuisance}, not which a proposed modal set can localise.

\subsubsection{Commissioning window: a structural prerequisite}\label{sec:res-comm}

Table~\ref{tab:burn} reports a sweep over commissioning length. The results are unforgiving. With 30 or 60 days of commissioning the monitor is invalid: false-alarm probabilities of 0.24 and 0.25 for PHASE (24 and 25 of 100 records), and 0.73 and 0.63 for the black-box variant. The mechanism is extrapolation. The bias discount $\hat\delta$ measures misspecification inside the observed covariate envelope, and a monitor commissioned in one season spends the rest of the year outside it. At 120 days validity returns (1 of 100) but detection collapses to zero, because the enlarged $\hat\delta$ consumes all the evidence a 5\% signal can generate. \begin{revblock}Between 180 and 240 days the range of temperature seen in commissioning covers 97--99\% of the monitoring epochs and there is no false alarm, yet the allowance is still large (0.12 and 0.09) and almost nothing is detected; at 300 days detection reaches 60\% and at 365 days 100\% with $\hat\delta=0.020$. Coverage of the covariate range is therefore necessary but not sufficient: the allowance keeps falling until the window spans the seasonal cycle, and $\hat\delta$ itself is the more informative diagnostic. With high coverage a large $\hat\delta$ makes the monitor valid but powerless, a visible failure; with low coverage, as at 30 and 60 days, an allowance of 0.07--0.08 measures only the bias inside the observed envelope and understates what the structure does outside it, and the monitor is invalid. Both diagnostics are needed.\end{revblock}

\subsubsection{Operational and environmental translation}\label{sec:res-oper}

The guarantee in Proposition~\ref{prop:valid} converts into an operational statement of unusual clarity \rev{when its conditions hold}. It is not a rate per epoch or per year but a bound over the monitoring life of one process: across a fleet of instrumented structures, at most a proportion $\alpha$ will ever raise a \rev{first} false alarm, however long they are monitored. At $\alpha=0.01$, a portfolio of 500 monitored buildings expects at most five spurious \rev{first-alarm} inspection triggers in total, not per year. \rev{This is conditional on commissioning coverage and the conditional mean bound; it concerns first alarms, and each restart after an investigation needs its own share of the budget.}

The contrast with threshold-based practice is stark. \begin{revblock}A $3\sigma$ chart exceeded its threshold at 7.6\% of epochs in the undamaged records. Under an inspect-and-resume rule that counts each exceedance as a trigger and ignores further exceedances for a lockout period after an inspection, the mean number of spurious triggers per structure-year was 111 with no lockout, 91 with a one-day lockout and 37 with a one-week lockout (60 records). A Shewhart chart has no memory, so resetting it after an inspection does not change its behaviour; the rates assume that the structure stays healthy and the chart resumes as before. They are about half the figure of 180 per year that follows from dividing a year by the two-day median time to a first alarm, which overstates the rate because a median is not a mean time between triggers.\end{revblock} Table~\ref{tab:carbon} translates the difference using a user-supplied mobilisation factor; we deliberately avoid asserting a carbon value for an inspection, since it depends on crew travel, access equipment and structure type, and asset owners should substitute locally derived factors. The arithmetic is linear and the conclusion is insensitive to the factor chosen: the avoided burden is dominated by the alarm rate, which differs across monitors by orders of magnitude.

\section{Application to the Z24 bridge}\label{sec:z24}

The simulation study above is controlled but synthetic. This section applies PHASE to real measurements from the Z24 progressive damage test, the canonical benchmark in vibration-based SHM \citep{peeters2001z24}, in which a three-span prestressed concrete highway overpass near Koppigen, Switzerland, was subjected to a documented sequence of structural interventions before demolition in 1998; the test programme is described in detail by \citet{reynders2009z24}.

\subsection{Data and modal feature extraction}

We use the released ambient-vibration records for the first ten test states: two undamaged reference measurements (4 and 9 August 1998), progressive lowering of the Koppigen pier by 20, 40, 80 and 95\,mm, a foundation tilt, a third reference measurement taken after the settlement had been reversed and the pier lifted back, and concrete spalling at the soffit over 12 and 24\,m$^2$. Accelerations were sampled at 100\,Hz in nine instrument setups per state; we use only the five permanently installed reference accelerometers, which are common to every setup and therefore free of the roving-sensor differences between setups. Both released measurement modalities are analysed: ambient vibration, driven by traffic and wind, and forced vibration, driven by shakers. The forced records have a better signal-to-noise ratio and give within-state coefficients of variation of 0.24--0.97\% against 0.35--1.4\% for the ambient records, so they serve as an independent check that the conclusions are not artefacts of one excitation type.

Each record is divided into non-overlapping windows of 8{,}192 samples (81.9\,s), each treated as one monitoring epoch. For each epoch the cross-power spectral density matrix of the five reference channels is formed by Welch's method and its first singular value taken, that is, frequency domain decomposition; three natural frequencies are then peak-picked in fixed bands with parabolic interpolation. This yields 690 ambient and 711 forced epochs across the ten states. The three tracked modes lie near 3.87, 9.82 and 12.7\,Hz and have within-state coefficients of variation of 0.35\%, 0.75\% and 1.4\%; other candidate bands were discarded because the automatic picker could not track them consistently across all ten states, and this restriction to $m=3$ features is a limitation of the analysis rather than of the data.

Figure~\ref{fig:bridge} shows the structure and the location of the intervention. Figure~\ref{fig:z24}(left) shows the resulting state means. The physics is clearly visible: all three frequencies fall monotonically through the settlement sequence, by 4.9\%, 1.3\% and 4.0\% respectively at 95\,mm, and all three recover when the settlement is reversed.

\subsection{What this dataset can and cannot test}\label{sec:z24-scope}

Two features of the released data constrain what may be concluded, and we state them before reporting results. First, no environmental covariate accompanies the ambient-vibration files, so the physics-structured surrogate of Section~3.3 degenerates to a predictable running intercept and the identifiability analysis cannot be exercised; correspondingly, the damage cone is instantiated at its coarsest physically justified level, namely that stiffness loss cannot raise a natural frequency, so the admissible set is the non-positive orthant in standardised log-frequency space. Localisation is not attempted, as no calibrated finite element model of the bridge is available to us.

Second, and more consequentially, each state was measured on a different day. The two undamaged reference measurements, taken five days apart, differ by 0.9\%, 1.4\% and 0.3\% in the three tracked modes, which is two to four times the within-state scatter. Test-day effects are therefore of the same order as the damage signatures, and no monitor can attribute a change to damage rather than to environment without a covariate. \begin{revblock}
The main results below treat the epochs within a state as exchangeable and are reported over 200 random orderings of the epochs within each block. That is an assumption, and we check it rather than assume it. For the ambient records, the median lag-one autocorrelation of the tracked log-frequencies within a state is $0.02$, and only 1 of the 30 state-by-mode series is significant at the 5\% level (3\%, close to chance); a trend test is significant for 13\% and a test for a set-up effect for 7\%. For the forced records the median lag-one autocorrelation is $0.36$ and 70\% of the series are significant, so exchangeability within a forced state is not supported. Section~\ref{sec:z24-results} therefore also reports re-analyses that keep the recorded order (chronological, reversed), a random circular shift of the recorded order, and shuffles of whole measurement set-ups with the order inside each set-up preserved. The 200 orderings are a resampling device for this one dataset: the reported standard errors are Monte Carlo errors over orderings, a rate of 0 or 1 means the outcome does not depend on the ordering, and none of it quantifies uncertainty about other bridges or independent monitoring campaigns, of which there is one.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Is the sign restriction appropriate for settlement, tilt and spalling?} The cone is derived for stiffness loss at fixed mass, and the Z24 interventions are pier settlement, foundation tilt and concrete spalling, which alter support conditions, load paths and cross-section as well as stiffness. The sign restriction is a modelling assumption, and for these interventions it is supported by the data only a posteriori. Relative to the pooled reference states, the three tracked frequencies fell in every damage state of both modalities from 80\,mm settlement onwards: by up to 5.5\%, 2.3\% and 4.0\% at 95\,mm, by 1.0--1.3\%, 1.2--1.9\% and 3.4--3.7\% under foundation tilt, and by 0.5--0.8\%, 0.8--1.2\% and 1.5--2.1\% under spalling. Only the third mode in the two smallest settlement states rose, by at most 0.6\%, comparable to the day-to-day offset. That agrees with the physical expectation that settlement and tilt release restraint and that spalling removes section stiffness faster than mass, but it does not justify the assumption in general. Mechanisms that can \emph{raise} some tracked frequencies, such as mass loss, crack closure under prestress or temperature, or boundary stiffening, lie outside the cone, and a net frequency increase caused by damage would not be flagged as damage. As implemented, the score is the maximum over singleton, pair and all-mode generators, so a pattern in which some frequencies fall and others rise still scores on the falling ones; it is only the rising components that are discarded. Two mitigations follow. The cone can be enlarged with generators for known mass-change mechanisms, at the cost of power. And an omnibus process (PHASE-$\Omega$ here) can run alongside as a separate \emph{change notification} channel with its own share of the budget, so that a frequency rise prompts engineering review without being reported as damage.
\end{revblock}

\paragraph{The commissioning envelope, confirmed on real data.} R1a commissions the monitor on the first reference measurement and then monitors the second, five days later, with no damage in between. Every method, PHASE included, alarms in every ordering under both excitation types. The estimated bias discount, $\hat\delta=0.13$--$0.14$, was calibrated within a single test day and does not cover between-day variation. R1b repeats the exercise with the commissioning window pooled across both reference days, \rev{with epochs from the two days mixed at random,} and monitoring held-out reference epochs: PHASE, its omnibus variant and the PCA chart then alarm in none of 200 orderings, the conformal martingale in 1\%, while the repeated $\chi^2$ test alarms in 14\% of ambient and 35\% of forced orderings, and CUSUM and Shiryaev--Roberts -- whose thresholds a practitioner without replicate healthy campaigns must calibrate on the commissioning window -- alarm in 86\% and 83\%. The simulation finding of Section~\ref{sec:res-comm}, that anytime-valid monitoring requires a commissioning window spanning the operational envelope and is actively misleading otherwise, thus reproduces on real measurements in both modalities.

\paragraph{Directional discrimination, confirmed on real data.} R3 is the real-data counterpart of the benign stiffening experiment. Commissioning on the 95\,mm settled state and then monitoring the state after the settlement had been reversed, PHASE alarmed in none of 200 orderings while the otherwise identical omnibus variant alarmed in all of them, at a median of 14 epochs. The $3\sigma$ chart, the repeated $\chi^2$ test, the PCA chart and the Mahalanobis index also alarmed in every ordering; the result is identical under forced excitation. The bridge really did change, and substantially: all three frequencies rose, by 4.6\%, 0.9\% and 2.7\%. A damage detector should not report damage when a structure recovers stiffness, and the physics-derived sign restriction is what supplies that discrimination. The conformal martingale, CUSUM, Shiryaev--Roberts and EWMA also stay silent here, but they consume the same cone score, so their immunity is inherited from the physics rather than earned by their own machinery. Because the restriction is directional, this conclusion is immune to the attribution difficulty discussed below: whatever caused the frequencies to rise, damage did not. \rev{It is also the one result that is insensitive to the ordering of the data: PHASE stayed silent and PHASE-$\Omega$ alarmed in every ordering of Table~\ref{tab:z24order}, in both modalities.}

\subsection{What the real-data study adds}\label{sec:z24-adds}

\begin{revblock}
Two claims of the paper survive contact with real measurements, and a third does not. The dependence of the monitor on its commissioning data is confirmed, as a failure mode of PHASE and not only of its competitors: a monitor commissioned within one test day alarms on the next, and the allowance estimated from commissioning data changes five- to eight-fold depending on whether the commissioning window contains one day or two kept apart. The value of restricting the alternative to physically admissible directions is confirmed in the cleanest possible form, on a genuine structural change of known sign, under every ordering and both excitation types. The claim that the cone-restricted process crosses the alarm threshold earlier than the omnibus process on a real damage sequence holds only when commissioning epochs from the two reference days are mixed, and is not claimed as a general finding. What the dataset cannot support, and what we therefore do not claim, is calibrated separation of damage from environmental variation: that would require a monitoring campaign with a synchronous covariate record, of which the Z24 long-term monitoring series and the KW51 campaign are the obvious candidates for future work. Of the components of PHASE, only the cone, the e-process and the allowance are exercised here, in reduced form (Table~\ref{tab:components}); the physics-structured surrogate, the nuisance subspace, identifiability-aware adaptation in its full form and localisation are validated by simulation only.
\end{revblock}

\section{Discussion and conclusions}\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Implications for practice}\label{sec:practice}

Three practical implications follow, each conditional on the assumptions stated.

\begin{revblock}
First, alarm governance can become a budget rather than a tuning exercise, \emph{if} commissioning covers the operating envelope and the conditional-mean bound holds. Under those conditions an asset owner can state that at most 1\% of monitored structures will ever raise a first false alarm. That is a candidate specification for a performance agreement, but the present evidence does not support it as an unconditional contractual commitment. The calibration procedure has not been shown to enforce the conditions (Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}; Section~4.2.6); the variant with a provable calibration step (PHASE-C) pays a large price in power; and the guarantee covers the first alarm of one monitoring process, so an agreement would have to define the commissioning protocol, a coverage test, the restart rule and how the budget is spent across restarts (for example by giving the $k$-th restart a share $\alpha_k$ with $\sum_k\alpha_k\le\alpha$). Because the guarantee is uniform over stopping rules, operators may watch the evidence process continuously without invalidating the alarm. That does not extend the guarantee to action rules set below the alarm threshold: by Ville's inequality the probability that the evidence ever reaches a level $w$ is at most $1/w$ under the null, so an inspection policy triggered at $W_t\ge10$ is protected only at the level $0.1$, not $0.01$.
\end{revblock}

Second, the detectability map belongs in the design phase. It is computed from the finite element model and the expected identification scatter, before installation, and states which substructures a proposed sensor and mode configuration can support claims about \rev{\emph{relative to the nuisance model assumed}}. In our eight-storey frame, moving from four to six identified modes raised the identifiable fraction at storeys 1 and 8 from 0.034 and 0.064 to 0.245 and 0.349, converting effectively blind substructures into monitorable ones. \rev{The map is not a localisation guarantee: storey~1 has a non-zero identifiable fraction and was nevertheless never localised correctly (Section~4.2.6), because localisation also needs the identifiable signatures of different storeys to be separable, and the map changes when the nuisance model changes (Table~\ref{tab:nuisance}).} This is a concrete, physics-derived criterion for instrumentation planning, and it integrates naturally with BIM-based asset models where the finite element model and sensor layout already coexist.

Third, the commissioning requirement should be treated as a design constraint rather than a nuisance. Our results indicate that a monitoring system commissioned over a single season is not merely less powerful but positively misleading. \rev{What is required is adequate coverage of the operating envelope; in the seasonal scenario simulated here a full calendar year was what achieved that, which is not a universal requirement. Section~6.2 sets out how coverage can be assessed in practice.} Where waiting a full year is unacceptable, the natural remedy is exactly the one this special issue is concerned with: use the calibrated digital twin to \emph{synthesise} the operational envelope, generating reference-condition modal responses across temperatures and load states that have not yet been observed, and calibrate $\hat\delta$ against simulated rather than measured excursions. This substitutes model risk for extrapolation risk, and quantifying that trade-off is a natural next step.

\begin{revblock}
\subsection{Deployment prerequisites and practical considerations}

\paragraph{Prerequisites.} PHASE needs a digital twin with modal sensitivities, at least $\dim\mathcal N+1$ reliably identified modes (more for localisation), synchronous measurements of the covariates the nuisance model uses, an automated modal identification chain with quality control, and a commissioning period that is declared complete only when the coverage checks below pass.

\paragraph{Judging commissioning coverage in advance.} Representativeness refers to the future, so it cannot be certified from commissioning data alone; the practical question is how to make the assumption checkable. We suggest, and have only partly tested, the following protocol. (i)~Declare the target envelope before commissioning from independent records, for example multi-year site climatology for temperature and the occupancy or traffic schedule for load, and measure the fraction of that envelope covered by the commissioning data. In the simulated sweep (Table~\ref{tab:burn}) validity returned only when coverage of the monitoring epochs was close to complete; at coverage around one half, the false-alarm probability was 0.24--0.25. (ii)~Estimate $\hat\delta$ separately on seasonal or operational blocks of commissioning data and treat a large spread as evidence that one block does not represent the others. (iii)~Report coverage online and flag, or gate, alarms issued outside the commissioning envelope. Gating by itself did not restore validity in our sweep (PHASE-E), so this is a diagnostic, not a remedy. (iv)~Treat the guarantee as inactive, and alarms as provisional, until declared coverage is reached; extend commissioning, as a restart with its own budget share, when a season or operating regime not yet seen occurs. (v)~Where the envelope cannot be observed, use the twin to synthesise it and state the model risk. None of these steps converts representativeness into a theorem; they make a failure visible earlier.

\paragraph{Missing data and recalibration.} Skipping an epoch leaves the evidence process unchanged provided the decision to skip is made without looking at that epoch's score (sensor outage, a scheduled quality rejection based on the identification's own uncertainty); screening epochs by the size of their residual would bias the process and void the guarantee. Dropped or mismatched modes were not simulated. Any re-estimation of the scatter $D$, of $\mu_0$ or of $\hat\delta$, and any re-commissioning after maintenance or a retrofit, starts a new process and should consume a pre-allocated share of the budget; known changes outside the cone, such as stiffening retrofits, should trigger planned re-commissioning.

\paragraph{Are the delays acceptable?} A median delay of 59 days for a 5\% loss at a single storey, and detection of only one record in three for a 2\% loss within nine months, are acceptable for slow deterioration but not for sudden events (impact, earthquake, scour), for which event-triggered inspection remains necessary. Whether a given delay is acceptable depends on the deterioration rate and on the consequence of delay for the structure, which the owner must judge; the delays reported here are the price of a 1\% lifetime budget and of the conservative allowance.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Sustainability implications}\label{sec:sust}

The sustainability case for instrumented infrastructure rests on the claim that continuous monitoring enables condition-based intervention: repairs made early, at small scale, in place of replacement. That claim depends on alarms being both trusted and timely. Our results indicate that the conventional charts \rev{implemented here} can satisfy neither simultaneously -- naive charts alarm constantly, and charts calibrated to stop alarming stop detecting -- while an anytime-valid rule \rev{can, in the simulated setting and conditionally on adequate commissioning}. The relevant environmental benefit \rev{would} therefore \rev{not be} primarily the avoided carbon of unnecessary inspections, real though that is, but the preservation of the embodied carbon in structures that are repaired rather than replaced because deterioration was detected while it was still small. \rev{That benefit is a hypothesis motivated by these results; this paper measures false-alarm and detection behaviour, not avoided replacements or avoided emissions.} Reviews of AI in the built environment tend to locate sustainability gains in energy optimisation and material efficiency \citep{bibri2025aidt}; the gain argued here is of a different kind, residing in the trustworthiness of the decisions a monitoring system supports. This connects to SDG 9 through resilient infrastructure, SDG 11 through the safe operation of the existing building stock, and SDG 13 through the emissions avoided by extending service life.

There is a further, less obvious \rev{possible} implication for equity. The commissioning result \rev{suggests} that rigorous monitoring requires patience rather than \rev{only} expensive hardware, and the physics-informed surrogate delivers usable behaviour from many fewer parameters than a black-box alternative \rev{during short commissioning, although with a long commissioning window the black-box surrogate matched it}. Both features \rev{could} favour deployment in rapidly urbanising contexts where instrumentation budgets are constrained but the building stock is young and its deterioration curve has not yet been characterised. \rev{These are plausible motivations, not demonstrated outcomes: the study contains no cost data, and a year of commissioning with covariate sensing and a calibrated twin is not free.}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The controlled evidence here is simulation-based and the real-data study is limited in the ways set out in Section~\ref{sec:z24-scope}: three tracked modes, no covariate record, no localisation, and states measured on different days so that detection cannot be attributed to damage rather than environment. The generator was constructed to be adversarial in specific ways -- unmodelled thermal lag and gradients, heavy-tailed identification noise, an imperfect load proxy -- but a shear frame is a simple structure, real EOV includes effects we did not model such as humidity, wind and freeze--thaw stiffening, and real modal identification produces mode-mismatching and dropouts that we did not simulate. The Z24 progressive damage test exercises the directional claim on real measurements and the commissioning claim only in part, and it cannot exercise the identifiability analysis; a campaign with synchronous temperature, such as the Z24 long-term monitoring series or the KW51 retrofitting campaign, is the necessary next step. \rev{The comparators are single implementations calibrated on simulated healthy campaigns; their behaviour here should not be generalised to environmental-normalisation methods as a class.}

Several limitations are methodological. Only frequencies are used; mode shapes and their derivatives carry localisation information that the cone construction could exploit directly. The null \eqref{eq:null} is stated as a conditional mean bound \rev{that the surrogate is meant to make true, not a property the physics guarantees}, and the weight falls on $\hat\delta$. \rev{The theorem holds conditionally; the implemented estimate of $\hat\delta$, two standard errors from a finite commissioning sample, has no guarantee of its own, and the provably calibrated variant PHASE-C is too conservative to be useful over a one-year window. The representativeness assumption that would close the gap cannot be verified from commissioning data.} Our commissioning sweep shows how the allowance fails when the envelope is not covered. \rev{The long healthy period costs power: after two years PHASE detected 5\% loss in two thirds of records and 2\% loss in none, and the restart variant that repairs this lost its empirical false-alarm control.} The detection delays reported are two to three times those of an oracle CUSUM, which is the price of the anytime guarantee, and for a structure monitored under a genuinely fixed horizon by an analyst who never peeks, the oracle procedure would be preferable. Finally, the localisation index is a heuristic accumulator rather than a calibrated estimator, \rev{with a baseline preference for some storeys on healthy data}; giving localisation claims their own anytime-valid guarantee via e-value based false discovery rate control across zones is a clear extension.

\subsection{Conclusions}\label{sec:concl}

Structural health monitoring systems make thousands of decisions per year using rules designed for one. This paper has shown that, for the charts implemented here, the resulting false-alarm probability is not a small correction -- a $3\sigma$ chart alarmed on every undamaged simulated structure within days -- and that raising thresholds to control error over one monitoring year traded the problem for a worse one, since the chart so calibrated detected no 5\% damage at all in our experiments.

PHASE addresses this by replacing the threshold with a betting e-process whose guarantee holds at every instant of an unbounded campaign, \rev{conditionally on a bound for the expected score of an undamaged structure}, and by using eigenvalue perturbation in four distinct roles: constraining the nuisance surrogate, restricting the alternative to a polyhedral damage cone, determining which stiffness-loss patterns are identifiable relative to the nuisance, and protecting identifiable damage from online adaptation. Across \rev{3{,}600 simulated records} it raised no false alarm on 300 undamaged structures at either a one- or a three-year horizon \rev{(upper 95\% limit 1.2\% in each)}, detected 5\% single-storey stiffness loss at storey~3 in every record within a median of 59 days \rev{(and 2\% loss in a third of records)}, localised damage correctly at storeys 3 and 6 but not at storey~1, and remained silent under a benign stiffening event that an otherwise identical omnibus detector flagged in every record. \rev{The observed false-alarm control belongs to the implemented, empirically calibrated rule; the theorem covers the conditional statement, and the provably calibrated variant has little power. Damage that starts after a long healthy period is detected more slowly and less often, and a restart variant that removes most of that penalty exceeded the nominal false-alarm level.}

On real measurements from the Z24 bridge \rev{the data support two narrow conclusions}: a monitor commissioned within a single test day false-alarms on the next undamaged day, and when the pier settlement was reversed and the bridge recovered stiffness, PHASE stayed silent in all 200 orderings while every omnibus detector reported damage in all of them. \rev{Its response to settlement depends on how commissioning epochs are ordered and is not evidence of separation between damage and environment.}

Two findings qualify the method and, we believe, matter more than the headline comparisons. \rev{Stiffness-loss patterns whose signature lies in the nuisance subspace -- uniform loss always, and for the frame studied here a four-dimensional family including equal loss at storeys 1 and 8 -- cannot be distinguished from temperature or load change using frequencies,} so a monitor should state which substructures it can and cannot speak about, \rev{and identifiability relative to the nuisance is not the same as localisability.} And calibrated monitoring requires a commissioning window spanning the operational envelope; below that, monitors are not conservative but actively misleading. Both limitations are consequences of the physics, and both are visible only because the physics was carried into the inference rather than left in the surrogate; \rev{the second is confirmed on Z24 and the first only in simulation.} The Z24 study adds a third: without a synchronous environmental covariate, no monitor can attribute a change to damage, and reporting detection rates on such data risks crediting the detector for the calendar.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Proofs}

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}.}
By \eqref{eq:shift} the standardised signature of a loss pattern $\rho$ is $-A\rho$. A uniform modulus change of relative size $\epsilon$ produces $-\tfrac12\epsilon D^{-1}\kappa$ and a relative added mass $\zeta$ produces $-\tfrac12\zeta D^{-1}\eta$, so the set of signatures attainable by the nuisance is exactly $\mathcal{N}$. Hence \rev{(a)} $\rho$ is confounded if and only if $A\rho\in\mathcal{N}$, that is $P_\perp A\rho=0$; the confounded patterns form $\mathcal K=\ker(P_\perp A)$, of dimension $n-\mathrm{rank}(P_\perp A)\ge n-\mathrm{rank}(P_\perp)=n-(m-\dim\mathcal N)$. \rev{(b)} For $\rho=\rho_0\mathbf{1}$, \eqref{eq:rowsum} gives $S\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}=\kappa$, so $A\rho=\rho_0D^{-1}\kappa\in\mathcal{N}$ for every $\rho_0$. \rev{(c) Suppose $A$ is injective and $\mathcal N\cap\mathrm{range}(A)=\mathrm{span}\{D^{-1}\kappa\}$. If $\rho\in\mathcal K$ then $A\rho\in\mathcal N\cap\mathrm{range}(A)$, so $A\rho=cA\mathbf 1$ (because $A\mathbf1=D^{-1}\kappa$), and injectivity gives $\rho=c\mathbf1$. Conversely, if $v\in\ker A$, $v\ne0$, then $Av=0\in\mathcal N$ so $v\in\mathcal K$, and $v\not\propto\mathbf1$ because $A\mathbf1=D^{-1}\kappa\ne0$; if $u=A\rho_u\in\mathcal N$ is not parallel to $D^{-1}\kappa$ then $\rho_u\in\mathcal K$ and $\rho_u\not\propto\mathbf1$. (d) $\rho$ and $\rho'$ have signatures that differ by something the nuisance cannot produce exactly when $P_\perp A(\rho-\rho')\ne0$, and the rank of $P_\perp A$ is at most the rank of $P_\perp$, which is $m-\dim\mathcal N$.} If $m\le\dim\mathcal{N}$ then generically $\mathcal{N}=\R^m$ and $P_\perp=0$, so no pattern is identifiable. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:poly}.}
The first claim holds because each $v_k\in\Cone$ has unit norm and $\|\Pi_\Cone(r)\|=\sup_{v\in\Cone,\|v\|\le1}\langle v,r\rangle$. For the second, let $v^\star$ attain the supremum and let $v_k$ satisfy $\angle(v_k,v^\star)\le\gamma$, so $\|v_k-v^\star\|=2\sin(\gamma/2)$. Then by Cauchy--Schwarz $\langle v_k,r\rangle=\langle v^\star,r\rangle+\langle v_k-v^\star,r\rangle\ge\|\Pi_\Cone(r)\|-2\sin(\gamma/2)\|r\|$. Monotonicity is immediate since enlarging $V$ enlarges the set over which the maximum is taken. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}.}
Fix $\lambda\in[0,1/\mu_0)$. Since $X_t\in[0,1]$, the factor $1+\lambda(X_t-\mu_0)\ge1-\lambda\mu_0>0$, so $W^\lambda_t\ge0$. The bet $\lambda$ is deterministic and $\mu_0$ is an $\F_0$-measurable constant, so
$\E[W^\lambda_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=W^\lambda_{t-1}\big(1+\lambda(\E[X_t\mid\F_{t-1}]-\mu_0)\big)\le W^\lambda_{t-1}$
by \eqref{eq:null} and $\lambda\ge0$. Thus each $W^\lambda$ is a non-negative supermartingale with initial value one, and so is the finite mixture $W_t$, convex combinations of supermartingales being supermartingales. Ville's inequality gives $\Prob(\exists t: W_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\E[W_{t_0}]\alpha=\alpha$. Nothing in the argument uses independence across epochs, the marginal law of $X_t$, or the accuracy of $\hat\beta_{t-1}$; only predictability of the bets and of the plug-in, which holds by construction, and the conditional mean bound \eqref{eq:null}. The same argument covers predictable bet sequences $\lambda_t$, since $\lambda_t$ then factors out of the conditional expectation. \hfill$\square$

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}.}
(i) Under $m_t\le\delta=\hat\delta$, $\E[1+\lambda(X_t-\mu_0)\mid\F_{t-1}]=1+\lambda m_t\le1+\lambda\delta$, so dividing the increment by the deterministic factor $1+\lambda\delta$ restores the supermartingale property; Ville's inequality applies as in Proposition~\ref{prop:valid}.

(ii) Let $\iota_s=\mathbf 1\{s<\sigma\}$. Since $m_s$ is $\F_{s-1}$-measurable and $\hat\delta$ is $\F_{t_0}$-measurable, $\{s<\sigma\}=\{m_u\le\hat\delta\ \forall\,t_0<u\le s\}\in\F_{s-1}$, so $\iota_s$ is predictable. Define $V^\lambda_t=\prod_{s=t_0+1}^t f_s$ with $f_s=\frac{1+\lambda(X_s-\mu_0)}{1+\lambda\hat\delta}$ if $\iota_s=1$ and $f_s=1$ otherwise. For $\iota_s=1$, $\E[f_s\mid\F_{s-1}]=(1+\lambda m_s)/(1+\lambda\hat\delta)\le1$; for $\iota_s=0$ it equals one. Hence $V^\lambda$, and the mixture $V$, are non-negative supermartingales with initial value one and $\Prob(\exists t:V_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha$. On $\{\sigma=\infty\}$ we have $V=\widetilde W$, so $\Prob(\exists t:\widetilde W_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha+\Prob(\sigma<\infty)$.

(iii) Put $\xi_s=X_s-\E[X_s\mid\F_{s-1}]$. Because $X_s\in[0,1]$, conditionally on $\F_{s-1}$ the variable $\xi_s$ lies in an interval of length one, so Azuma--Hoeffding (conditional Hoeffding lemma) gives $\Prob\big(-n^{-1}\sum_{s\in C}\xi_s>u\big)\le e^{-2nu^2}$. With $u=\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)/(2n)}$, with probability at least $1-\varepsilon$ we have $\bar m_C=\bar X_C-\mu_0-n^{-1}\sum_{s\in C}\xi_s\le\bar X_C-\mu_0+u$, hence $\bar m_C\le\hat\delta_\varepsilon$ (the truncation at zero cannot bind against a negative upper bound on $\bar m_C$). On that event representativeness gives $\sup_{t>t_0}m_t\le\bar m_C\le\hat\delta_\varepsilon$, so $\sigma=\infty$. Thus $\Prob(\sigma<\infty)\le\varepsilon$, and part~(ii) at level $\alpha-\varepsilon$ gives $\mathrm{FWFA}\le(\alpha-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon=\alpha$. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:delay}.}
Write $Z_s=\log(1+\lambda(X_s-\mu_0))-\log(1+\lambda\hat\delta)$, so that $L^\lambda_t=L^\lambda_{t^\star}+\sum_{s=t^\star+1}^tZ_s$ for $t>t^\star$. The increments are bounded: $Z_s\le B_\lambda$ and $Z_s\ge\log(1-\lambda\mu_0)-\log(1+\lambda\hat\delta)>-\infty$ because $\lambda<1/\mu_0$. Since the mixture dominates its components, $\widetilde W_t\ge\widetilde W^\lambda_t/|\Lambda|$, so $L^\lambda_t\ge b$ implies $\widetilde W_t\ge1/\alpha$ and hence $\tau\le T:=\inf\{t>t^\star:L^\lambda_t\ge b\}$. Let $e_s=\E[Z_s\mid\F_{s-1}]\ge\gamma_\lambda$ and $\xi_s=Z_s-e_s$, a bounded martingale difference sequence. For $n>t^\star$, $T_n=T\wedge n$ is a bounded stopping time, so optional stopping gives
\[
\E\Big[\sum_{s=t^\star+1}^{T_n}Z_s\,\Big|\,\F_{t^\star}\Big]=\E\Big[\sum_{s=t^\star+1}^{T_n}e_s\,\Big|\,\F_{t^\star}\Big]\ \ge\ \gamma_\lambda\,\E[T_n-t^\star\mid\F_{t^\star}].
\]
Before $T$ the log-wealth is below $b$, and at $T$ it exceeds $b$ by at most $B_\lambda$, so $L^\lambda_{T_n}\le b+B_\lambda$ and $\sum_{s=t^\star+1}^{T_n}Z_s=L^\lambda_{T_n}-L^\lambda_{t^\star}\le b+B_\lambda+(L^\lambda_{t^\star})^-$. Combining, $\gamma_\lambda\E[T_n-t^\star\mid\F_{t^\star}]\le b+B_\lambda+(L^\lambda_{t^\star})^-$; letting $n\to\infty$ (monotone convergence) bounds $\E[T-t^\star\mid\F_{t^\star}]$, and $(\tau-t^\star)^+\le T-t^\star$ gives \eqref{eq:delay}. The condition $\gamma_\lambda>0$ is exactly the requirement that the damage generate evidence faster than the allowance consumes it. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proposition~\ref{prop:restart}.}
For $t\le s_k$ the component $\widetilde W^{(k)}$ is constant at one, and for $t>s_k$ it is, by the argument of Proposition~\ref{prop:delta}(i) applied from $s_k$ with the conditioning $\F_{s_k}$, a non-negative supermartingale. A sum with weights $\pi_k$ of non-negative supermartingales (monotone convergence for infinitely many terms) is a non-negative supermartingale, with initial value $\sum_k\pi_k\le1$, and Ville's inequality gives $\Prob(\exists t:W^R_t\ge1/\alpha)\le\alpha\sum_k\pi_k\le\alpha$. For the delay, $W^R_t\ge\pi_{k^\star}\widetilde W^{(k^\star)}_t$, so an alarm occurs no later than the first time the log-wealth of component $k^\star$, mixed over $\Lambda$, reaches $\log(1/\alpha)+\log(1/\pi_{k^\star})$, and the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:delay} applies with $b$ replaced by $b+\log(1/\pi_{k^\star})$. \hfill$\square$
\end{revblock}
